## Intent

- Apply Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws to a switched circuit, one interval at a time.
- Compute how much an inductor's current and a capacitor's voltage change under a constant voltage or current.
- Explain why inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot change instantly, and what that means for a switch.

## Theory

### Kirchhoff's laws

The currents entering a node sum to zero (Kirchhoff's current law), and the
voltages around a closed loop sum to zero (Kirchhoff's voltage law)
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 2" />. A switching converter is a
different linear circuit in each interval of the switching period. Within
one interval both laws apply as usual, and the element laws below join the
intervals together <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.

### The inductor under a constant voltage

The voltage across an inductor is its inductance times the rate of change of
its current <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 6" />. With a constant
voltage across it, the current changes at a constant rate, that is,
linearly in time:

<Eq id="ind.di" />

This is the whole change during the interval. The converter pages use the
half-ripple $\Delta i_L$, which is half of the peak-to-peak change of the
inductor current (Erickson's convention).

### Stored energy

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field, a capacitor in its electric
field <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 6" />:

<Eq id="ind.E" />

<Eq id="cap.E" />

The current through an inductor cannot change instantly, and neither can the
voltage across a capacitor: a jump would change the stored energy in zero
time <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 6" />. When a transistor turns off,
the inductor current therefore has to continue somewhere, and a converter
gives it a path through a diode or a second switch
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />. In steady state the current also
ends every period where it started, which is
[volt-second balance](../../02-theory/volt-second-charge-balance/).

proportion to the inductance. This is why a larger inductor gives a smaller
current ripple at the same voltages and switching frequency.

## Bench exercise

Drive a resistor in series with an inductor from a function generator with a
square wave whose period is much shorter than the inductance divided by the
resistance. The inductor current, seen with a current probe, is then close to
a triangle: during each half-period the inductor sees an almost constant
voltage. Measure the slope of each ramp and compare it with the inductor
equation at the measured voltage across the inductor. Repeat with a capacitor
in place of the inductor, a period much shorter than the resistance times
the capacitance, and the voltage across the capacitor.

## Gotchas

- **The half-ripple is half.** The change on this page is the whole change
  during the interval; the converter pages' $\Delta i_L$ is half of the
  peak-to-peak ripple. Mixing the two conventions doubles or halves a result.
- **Never leave an inductor's current without a path.** Its current cannot
  stop instantly <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 6" />, so opening the
  only path forces a large voltage across the switch until something
  conducts.
- **A discharged capacitor across a stiff source draws a surge.** Its voltage
  cannot jump <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 6" />, so connecting it
  directly to a voltage source draws a current limited only by the resistance
  in the loop.
