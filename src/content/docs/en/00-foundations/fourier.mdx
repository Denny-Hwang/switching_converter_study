## Intent

- Split a periodic waveform into its dc component and its harmonics.
- Compute the harmonic amplitudes and the rms value of a rectangular pulse train, the waveform at a converter's switch node.
- Explain why a low-pass filter recovers the dc component of the switch-node voltage.

## Theory

### Periodic signals as sums of sinusoids

A periodic signal is the sum of its average value, the dc component, and
sinusoids at integer multiples of its fundamental frequency, the harmonics
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 17" />. The amplitudes of the
harmonics, plotted against frequency, form the signal's amplitude spectrum
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 17" />.

### The rectangular pulse train

The switch node of a buck converter carries a pulse train: the input voltage
for the fraction $D$ of every period and zero for the rest
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />. Its dc component is its average
(<EqRef id="sw.v_avg" label="average switch-node voltage" />), and its
harmonics have the amplitudes

<Eq id="fourier.pulse.harm" />

The amplitudes fall in inverse proportion to the harmonic order, and a
harmonic vanishes whenever $h D$ is an integer: at $D = 0.5$ all the even
harmonics are missing.

### The rms value

The rms value is the square root of the mean of the square over one period
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 11" />. For the pulse train only the
pulse contributes to that mean:

<Eq id="fourier.pulse.rms" />

The loss in a resistance depends on the rms value of its current, not on the
average (<EqRef id="loss.cond" label="conduction loss" />), and for a narrow
pulse the rms value is much larger than the average.

### Why the output filter works

A buck converter's L-C filter passes the dc component of the switch-node
voltage and attenuates its harmonics, the lowest of which lies at the
switching frequency. What remains at the output is the average, $D V_g$,
plus a small ripple <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.

## Bench exercise

On a buck evaluation board, measure the switch-node voltage with a
differential probe, or a probe with a short ground connection, and use the
oscilloscope's FFT function. Read the dc value and the amplitudes of the
first few harmonics, and compare them with the equations at the measured
duty ratio and input voltage. Check whether the oscilloscope displays each
harmonic's peak amplitude or its rms value, which is smaller by a factor of
$\sqrt{2}$.

## Gotchas

- **The harmonics do not stop at the switching frequency.** Those of an ideal
  pulse train fall only as $1/h$, so they extend far above the switching
  frequency; this matters for filters, interference and sampling.
- **Average and rms are different.** Two currents with the same average can
  have very different rms values, and resistive losses follow the rms value
  <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 11" />.
- **The rms value of a sinusoid is its amplitude divided by $\sqrt{2}$; that of
  a pulse train is not.** Use the rms value of the actual waveform
  <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 11" />.
