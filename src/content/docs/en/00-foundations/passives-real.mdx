## Intent

- Estimate the conduction loss in a winding or in a capacitor's series resistance from the rms current.
- Explain where an inductor's self-resonance comes from, and why an inductance measured near it is wrong.
- Estimate the output ripple added by a capacitor's series resistance.

## Theory

### Series resistance and conduction loss

A real inductor has the resistance of its winding
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />, and a real capacitor an
equivalent series resistance (ESR) <Cite key="ti_slva630" />. A current
through a resistance dissipates power in proportion to the square of its rms
value <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />:

<Eq id="loss.cond" />

The same equation gives the conduction loss of a transistor's on-resistance
(see the [semiconductors page](../semiconductors/)). It is the rms value that
counts, not the average: a pulsed current loses more than a steady one with
the same average (<EqRef id="fourier.pulse.rms" label="rms of a pulse train" />).

### Self-resonance

Neighbouring turns of a winding are separated by insulation, so a small
capacitance appears across each turn <Cite key="ti_snoa930" />. Together
these act as a capacitance in parallel with the inductance, and the two
resonate:

<Eq id="passive.f_srf" />

Below this frequency the component is inductive; above it the parallel
capacitance dominates and the component behaves as a capacitor
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 14" />. Wire-wound inductors with many
turns can have a high inductance but often a low self-resonant frequency
<Cite key="ti_snoa930" />. The self-resonant frequency can be measured with an
impedance analyzer: it is the frequency at which the phase of the impedance
crosses zero <Cite key="ti_snoa930" />.

### Capacitor series resistance and output ripple

A converter's output capacitor carries the ripple current through its
series resistance as well as through its capacitance, and the series
resistance adds a ripple voltage in phase with that current
<Cite key="ti_slva630" />:

<Eq id="cap.esr.ripple" />

This term adds to the capacitive ripple
(<EqRef id="buck.ripple.v" label="buck output ripple" />), and the
capacitor's series inductance (ESL) adds steps at the switching edges
<Cite key="ti_slva630" />. The two terms peak at different instants, so the
sum of their peak-to-peak values is an upper bound on the total.

## Bench exercise

With an impedance analyzer, or an LCR meter whose test frequency can be
stepped, measure the impedance magnitude and phase of a wound inductor with
many turns over frequency. Find the self-resonant frequency where the phase
crosses zero <Cite key="ti_snoa930" />, and estimate the parasitic capacitance
from the equation above. Then note how the inductance reading at a single
test frequency drifts as that frequency approaches the resonance.

## Gotchas

- **Measure an inductance well below its self-resonance.** Near the
  resonance the reading is neither the inductance nor the capacitance, and
  for windings with many turns the resonance can be low
  <Cite key="ti_snoa930" />.
- **The rms current sets the loss.** A winding or a series resistance that
  carries a pulsed current loses more than its average current suggests.
- **Edges add their own ripple.** The capacitor's series inductance produces
  steps in the output voltage at every switching edge, which neither the
  capacitive nor the resistive term predicts <Cite key="ti_slva630" />.
