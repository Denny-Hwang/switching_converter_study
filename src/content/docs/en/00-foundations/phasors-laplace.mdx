## Intent

- Represent sinusoidal signals by phasors, and inductors and capacitors by impedances.
- Compute the impedance of an inductor and a capacitor at a given frequency.
- Find a first-order low-pass filter's corner frequency and its attenuation above the corner, the building block of converter transfer functions.

## Theory

### Phasors and impedance

In sinusoidal steady state every voltage and current in a linear circuit is
a sinusoid at the source frequency. Each can be represented by a complex
number, its phasor, which carries the amplitude and the phase
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 9" />. Differentiation becomes
multiplication by $j\omega$, and each element is described by its impedance,
the ratio of its voltage phasor to its current phasor
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 9" />. For an inductor and a capacitor
the magnitudes are

An inductor's impedance rises with frequency and a capacitor's falls. That
is what lets a converter's L-C filter pass the dc component of the switch-node
voltage while it removes the switching harmonics
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.

### The Laplace transform and transfer functions

The Laplace transform extends the same idea from sinusoids to any signal:
with the complex frequency $s$ in place of $j\omega$, a linear circuit is
described by transfer functions, ratios of an output transform to an input
transform <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 15" />. Evaluated at
$j\omega$, a transfer function gives the frequency response, the gain and the
phase at each frequency <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 14" />.

### The first-order low-pass filter

A resistor in series with a capacitor, with the output taken across the
capacitor, is the simplest low-pass filter. Its gain is close to one at low
frequency and falls above the corner frequency
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 14" />:

Well above the corner, every decade of frequency lowers the gain by a factor
of ten: a slope of -20 dB per decade on a Bode plot
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 14" />. A converter's L-C filter is of
second order, and above its resonance it falls at -40 dB per decade
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" /> (see the

Sweep $f$ on its logarithmic axis: the gain is flat below the corner, equals
$1/\sqrt{2}$ at the corner and falls tenfold per decade above it. Then open
the capacitor's impedance and sweep $f$ again to see the same inverse
proportion.

## Bench exercise

Build an R-C low-pass filter with a corner in the audio range, drive it from
a function generator with a sine wave, and measure the output amplitude and
phase with an oscilloscope at several frequencies around the corner. Plot
the gain in decibels against frequency on a logarithmic axis and compare it
with the equation: at the corner the gain is down by 3 dB and the output
lags the input by 45°, and a decade above it the gain is down by about
20 dB.

## Gotchas

- **Magnitudes hide the phase.** An inductor and a capacitor with equal
  impedance magnitudes cancel in series instead of adding: at that frequency
  they are in series resonance <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 14" />.
- **Radians or hertz.** $\omega$ is in radians per second and $f$ in hertz;
  mixing them moves a corner by a factor of $2\pi$.
- **A loaded filter is a different filter.** The corner-frequency equation
  assumes that nothing draws current from the output. A load in parallel
  with the capacitor lowers the gain and moves the corner.
