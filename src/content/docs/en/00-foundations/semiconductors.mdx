## Intent

- Model a MOSFET that is on as a resistance, and a conducting diode as a forward voltage plus a small resistance.
- Estimate the capacitive turn-on loss and the gate-drive power, and see how both scale with the switching frequency.
- Tell the losses that grow with the switching frequency from those that grow with the load.

## Theory

### The switches of a converter

An ideal switch has no voltage across it when on and no current through it
when off. A converter realizes its switches with transistors, such as
MOSFETs, and diodes <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />. A transistor is
turned on and off by its gate; a diode switches by itself, conducting when
it is forward-biased and blocking when it is reverse-biased
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />.

### Conduction loss

A MOSFET that is fully on behaves as a resistance, its on-resistance, so its
conduction loss follows from its rms current
(<EqRef id="loss.cond" label="conduction loss" />). A conducting diode is
modelled by a constant forward voltage in series with a small resistance
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />:

<Eq id="loss.diode" />

The forward voltage takes a fixed voltage from the circuit whatever the
current, so it costs the most efficiency when the output voltage is low
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />.

### Switching loss from the node capacitance

When the transistor turns on, it discharges the capacitance at the switch
node, which includes its own output capacitance. The energy stored in that
capacitance is lost in every period <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />:

<Eq id="loss.sw.cap" />

In addition, the current and the voltage overlap for a short time during
each transition, and the diode's stored charge must be removed when it turns
off; both add loss at every switching event
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />.

### Gate-drive power

To switch a MOSFET, the driver moves the gate charge from its supply into the
gate and then back to ground, once per period. The power this takes is
dissipated in the driver and the gate resistances, not in the MOSFET's
channel <Cite key="balogh2018" />:

## Bench exercise

On a buck evaluation board whose switching frequency can be set, measure the
input and output power at a fixed load with a power analyzer or with a
multimeter pair on each side, at two switching frequencies. The increase in
loss divided by the increase in frequency estimates the energy lost per
switching period; compare it with the capacitive-loss and gate-drive
equations, using the capacitance and gate charge from the data sheets.

## Gotchas

- **Losses that happen once per period scale with frequency.** A higher
  switching frequency shrinks the inductor needed for a given ripple, but the
  capacitive, transition and gate-drive losses rise in proportion
  <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />.
- **Gate power heats the driver, not the MOSFET.** Budget it in the driver's
  dissipation <Cite key="balogh2018" />.
- **A diode drop is costly at low output voltage.** The same forward voltage
  is a larger fraction of a low output voltage, which is why low-voltage
  converters often replace the diode by a transistor, a synchronous
  rectifier <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 4" />.
