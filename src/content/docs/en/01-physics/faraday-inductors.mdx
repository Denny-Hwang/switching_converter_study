## Intent

- Relate a winding's current to the magnetic field intensity in its core (Ampère's law).
- Compute the flux-density swing from the voltage applied to a winding and its duration (Faraday's law).
- Explain why the flux swing in a converter's magnetic components is set by volt-seconds, not by the load current.

## Theory

### Ampère's law

The magnetic field intensity integrated around a closed path equals the
current that the path encloses. For $N$ turns on a core without a gap, with
the field uniform along the effective path length, this gives

### Faraday's law

A changing flux through a winding induces a voltage equal to the number of
turns times the rate of change of the flux. With the flux density uniform
over the core's effective area, a constant voltage applied to the winding
for the on-time changes the flux density linearly
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />, <Cite key="dixon2001" />:

<Eq id="mag.dB_faraday" />

This is the magnetic counterpart of the inductor law on the
[circuit-laws page](../../00-foundations/circuit-laws/): a constant voltage
makes the current ramp there, and the flux density here.

### Volt-seconds set the flux swing

The flux swing depends only on the volt-seconds applied, the turns and the
core area, not on the current drawn by the load. In a transformer, which
carries no dc magnetizing current, the volt-seconds alone set the flux. In a
filter inductor, the dc current sets the average flux density and the
volt-seconds set the swing around it
(<EqRef id="mag.B_pk" label="peak flux density" />). In steady state,
volt-second balance brings the flux back to its starting value in every
period (<EqRef id="vsb.v_off" label="volt-second balance" />).

## Bench exercise

Drive a winding on a ferrite core with a square wave from a function
generator through a small series resistor, and wind a second winding of a
few turns on the same core as a sense winding. The oscilloscope's integral of
the sense-winding voltage, divided by its turns and the core area, is the
flux density. Check that its peak-to-peak swing follows the equation above,
then double the drive frequency and check that the swing halves.

## Gotchas

- **Turns cut both ways.** At a given voltage, more turns lower the flux
  swing; at a given current, more turns raise the field intensity and the
  flux density (<EqRef id="mag.H_ampere" label="Ampère's law" />).
- **Net volt-seconds walk the flux into saturation.** A winding with a
  nonzero average voltage over a period has a flux that grows every period
  until the core saturates <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 6" />.
