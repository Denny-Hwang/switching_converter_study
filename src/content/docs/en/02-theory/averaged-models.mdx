## Intent

- Build the averaged dc model of a converter and add the inductor's winding resistance to it.
- Predict how that resistance limits the boost converter's conversion ratio and efficiency.
- Describe the DCM switch network as a loss-free resistor and compute its input resistance.

## Theory

### From switched circuit to dc model

Averaging every circuit equation over one switching period, with the
small-ripple approximation, removes the switching action: the averaged
inductor-voltage and capacitor-current equations are those of a dc circuit.
In that circuit the switch network acts as an ideal **dc transformer**
whose turns ratio is the conversion ratio. It passes power from input to
output without loss, and every loss mechanism appears as an ordinary
resistor or voltage drop around it <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />.
One model then gives the output voltage, the currents and the efficiency.

### Boost with inductor winding resistance

Add the inductor's winding resistance $R_L$ to the boost. Volt-second balance
gains the drop across $R_L$ in both intervals, while charge balance is
unchanged; solving the two together gives
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />:

<Eq id="boost.ccm.M_RL" />

The <EqRef id="boost.ccm.M" label="ideal boost ratio" /> is multiplied by a
factor that falls towards zero as $D$ approaches 1: the inductor carries the input
current, which grows much faster than the output current as $D$ rises, so the
loss in $R_L$ eventually takes all the extra input power. Instead of growing
without bound, the conversion ratio reaches a maximum and then falls. The
efficiency follows from the same model:

<Eq id="boost.ccm.eta_RL" />

$R_L$ is compared with $(1 - D)^2 R$, the load resistance reflected through
the dc transformer to the inductor's side. At high $D$ this reflected
resistance is small, so even a small winding resistance costs efficiency.
The derivation is on the [derivations page](../derivations/#deriv-averaging).

### DCM: the loss-free resistor

In DCM the inductor current starts from zero in every period. The energy
stored during the on-interval, and so the power drawn from the input, then
depends only on the input voltage, the duty ratio, the inductance and the
switching frequency. For the buck-boost and the flyback, with the
magnetizing inductance $L_M$:

<Eq id="dcm.P_in" />

The average input current is therefore proportional to the input voltage,
and the input port behaves like a resistor
<Cite key="singer1990" /> <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15" />:

<Eq id="lfr.R_in" />

This resistor does not dissipate the power it absorbs: it passes that power
to the output. The averaged DCM switch network is therefore modelled as a
**loss-free resistor**, a property that energy-harvesting interfaces use to
present a resistive load to a source. The
[source matcher](../../design/source-matcher/) matches a flyback's loss-free
resistor to a linear source.

## Bench exercise

On a low-power boost converter supplied from a current-limited bench supply,
measure the inductor's dc resistance with a four-wire method first. Then, at
a fixed resistive load, step the duty ratio up in open loop while you
measure the input and output power with multimeters. Plot the conversion
ratio and the efficiency against $D$ and compare them with the two equations
above, using your measured $R_L$. The switch and diode add further losses,
so the measured efficiency will sit below the prediction.

## Gotchas

- **Past the maximum, more duty gives less output.** Beyond the peak of the
  ratio, raising $D$ lowers $V$. A feedback loop that assumes the opposite
  sees its gain change sign there.
- **$R_L$ is the dc winding resistance only.** Core loss, ac winding loss and
  switching loss are not in this model; each adds its own loss element
  <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />.
- **The loss-free resistor holds only in DCM.** Once the current no longer
  returns to zero in each period, the input current is no longer
  proportional to the input voltage.
