## Intent

- Model a pulse-width modulator (PWM) and compute its small-signal gain.
- Compute the loop gain of a voltage-mode feedback loop at a given frequency.
- Quantify how feedback suppresses disturbances, and recognise where it stops helping.

## Theory

### The PWM modulator

A voltage-mode controller compares a control voltage $V_\mathrm{ctrl}$ with a
sawtooth ramp of peak amplitude $V_M$. The switch is on while the ramp is
below the control voltage, so the duty ratio is proportional to the control
voltage:

<Eq id="pwm.d" />

The modulator's small-signal gain, from control voltage to duty ratio, is
therefore $1/V_M$ <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 7" />.

### The loop gain

In a voltage-mode loop the output voltage is measured by a sensor of gain
$H$ (often a resistive divider) and compared with a reference. The
difference is amplified by the compensator $G_c$ and turned into a duty
ratio by the modulator, and the converter's control-to-output transfer
function $G_{vd}$ closes the loop. The **loop gain** is the product of the
gains around the loop <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 9" />:

<Eq id="loop.T" />

At each frequency the magnitudes multiply and the phases add. For the buck,
$G_{vd}$ is the transfer function of the [small-signal page](../small-signal/);
for the boost and buck-boost it includes the
[right-half-plane zero](../rhp-zero/).

### What feedback buys

A disturbance that enters the loop, from the input voltage or from the load,
reaches the output divided by $1 + T$ <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 9" />:

<Eq id="loop.suppression" />

Where the loop gain is large (at low frequencies), disturbances are
suppressed by roughly the loop gain, and the output follows the reference
divided by $H$: it depends on the sensor and the reference, not on the
converter's gain. Where the loop gain is much smaller than 1 (above the
crossover frequency), feedback has no effect. Near the crossover the loop
gain is complex; with a small phase margin the magnitude of $1 + T$ drops
below 1, and disturbances there are amplified rather than suppressed
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 9" />.

## Bench exercise

Measure the loop gain of a closed-loop converter by injection. Insert a small
resistor in the feedback path, between the output and the sensing divider,
and inject a small sinusoidal signal across it through an isolation
transformer. The ratio of the signals on the two sides of the resistor,
measured with a frequency response analyzer, is the loop gain
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 9" />. Find the crossover frequency and
the phase margin, then compare how the output responds to an input-voltage
step with the loop's suppression at low frequency.

## Gotchas

- **The modulator gain depends on the ramp amplitude.** A controller whose
  ramp amplitude scales with the input voltage makes the buck's loop gain
  independent of the input voltage, since the buck's dc gain equals the
  input voltage (<EqRef id="buck.ss.Gd0" />).
- **Use the complex loop gain away from dc.** The real-valued form above is
  exact only where $T$ is real. Elsewhere, divide by the magnitude of the
  complex sum $1 + T$; where the loop gain is large, the two agree closely.
- **A saturated modulator opens the loop.** Once the control voltage leaves
  the ramp's range, the duty ratio is clamped and the loop gain collapses;
  the equations on this page no longer apply.
