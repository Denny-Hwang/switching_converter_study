## Intent

- Explain physically why the boost and the buck-boost have a right-half-plane (RHP) zero.
- Compute the RHP zero and the double-pole parameters of both converters from circuit values.
- Know how the RHP zero limits the crossover frequency of a feedback loop.

## Theory

### Why the output first moves the wrong way

In the boost, energy reaches the output only while the diode conducts, that
is, during the off-interval. A step increase of the duty ratio lengthens the
on-interval and shortens the off-interval. Eventually the inductor current
rises and the output rises with it. Immediately after the step, however, the
inductor current has not changed yet while the diode conducts for a shorter
part of each period, so the average diode current, and the output voltage,
first fall <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />. The buck-boost behaves
the same way.

A zero in the right half of the $s$-plane produces exactly this: its
magnitude rises with frequency like that of an ordinary zero, but its phase
lags like that of a pole.

### Consequences for control

The RHP zero moves down in frequency as the load gets heavier (smaller $R$)
and as $D$ increases, so its lowest frequency occurs at full load and minimum
input voltage. Because it adds phase lag while the magnitude keeps rising,
the loop crossover frequency has to stay well below the lowest $\omega_z$ over
the operating range <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />. A compensator
cannot cancel the zero: that would take a pole in the right half-plane,
which is unstable.

## Bench exercise

On an open-loop boost at low power, apply a small step to the duty ratio,
for example by stepping the modulator's control input. Watch the output
voltage with AC coupling, triggered on the step: it dips briefly before it
rises to its new value. Repeat at a heavier load and compare the size of
the dip.

## Gotchas

- **Design for the worst case.** The lowest RHP-zero frequency occurs at the
  heaviest load and the highest duty ratio, that is, the lowest input voltage.
- **The buck has no RHP zero.** Its duty ratio acts on the switch node, which
  feeds the output filter directly; the boost and buck-boost deliver energy
  to the output only during the off-interval <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />.
- **Phase margin estimates must include the zero.** Its phase lag starts
  well below $\omega_z$, over the same frequencies where an ordinary zero's
  phase lead would start.
