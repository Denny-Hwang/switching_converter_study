## Intent

- Explain what a small-signal model is and when it is valid.
- Describe the buck converter's control-to-output transfer function: dc gain, double pole and quality factor.
- Compute $G_{d0}$, $\omega_0$ and $Q$ from the circuit values and predict the resonant peak.

## Theory

### Perturb and linearize

The averaged model of a converter is nonlinear, because the duty ratio
multiplies voltages and currents. To design a feedback loop we need its
response to small variations about the operating point. Each quantity is
written as its dc value plus a small ac variation: the duty ratio, for
example, as $D$ plus $\hat{d}(t)$. Products of two ac variations are
dropped. What remains is a linear circuit, the **small-signal model**, valid
for variations much smaller than the dc values and for frequencies well below
the switching frequency <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 7" />.

### The buck's control-to-output transfer function

In the buck, the duty ratio acts directly on the switch-node voltage, which
the L-C filter and the load then filter. The control-to-output transfer
function $G_{vd}(s)$, from $\hat{d}$ to the output voltage variation, is
therefore a dc gain followed by a second-order low-pass response: a pair of
poles at the resonant angular frequency $\omega_0$ of the filter, with a
quality factor $Q$ set by the load <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />.

### Reading the Bode plot

Well below $\omega_0$ the magnitude is flat at $G_{d0}$ and the phase is
near zero. At $\omega_0$ the magnitude is exactly $Q$ times the dc gain and
the phase has fallen by 90°; above $\omega_0$ the magnitude falls with the
square of frequency and the phase approaches −180°
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />. A light load (large $R$) gives a
high $Q$: a tall, narrow resonant peak and an abrupt phase change, which the
feedback loop has to accommodate. For hertz, divide $\omega_0$ by $2\pi$.

## Bench exercise

With a frequency response analyzer, inject a small sinusoidal variation into
the control input of an open-loop buck's PWM modulator and measure the ratio
of output-voltage variation to control-input variation over frequency. Find
the resonant peak and compare its frequency with $\omega_0/(2\pi)$. Repeat at
a lighter load and confirm that the peak grows while its frequency stays put.
Keep the injected amplitude small enough that the response does not change
when you halve it.

## Gotchas

- **Real components damp the resonance.** The ideal model has no loss other
  than the load. Winding resistance and capacitor series resistance lower $Q$,
  and the capacitor's series resistance adds a zero: its impedance is
  $1/(sC)$ plus that resistance.
- **The model is only valid well below the switching frequency** and for
  small variations <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 7" />. Near $f_s$ the
  averaged model no longer describes the circuit.
- **DCM changes the transfer function.** In DCM the inductor current no
  longer carries a state from one period to the next, and the L-C resonance
  disappears from the low-frequency response
  <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15" />.
