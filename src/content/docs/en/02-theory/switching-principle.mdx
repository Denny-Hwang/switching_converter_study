## Intent

- Explain why an ideal switching converter dissipates no power, unlike a linear regulator.
- Compute the dc component of a switch-node waveform from the duty ratio $D$.
- State what the output filter must do and what the small-ripple approximation assumes.

## Theory

### Why switch?

A linear regulator lowers a voltage by dropping the difference across a
transistor that carries the whole load current; that difference times the
load current is lost as heat. An ideal switch loses nothing: when it is on,
the voltage across it is zero, and when it is off, the current through it is
zero, so the product of the two, the power it dissipates, is zero in both
states <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 1 (Introduction)" />. Ideal
inductors and capacitors store and return energy without dissipating any.
A converter built only from ideal switches and reactive elements can
therefore convert power without loss.

### Filtering: keep the average, reject the harmonics

A Fourier series splits $v_s(t)$ into that dc component plus harmonics at
multiples of the switching frequency $f_s$. An L-C low-pass filter whose
corner frequency lies well below $f_s$ passes the dc component and strongly
attenuates the harmonics, so the output voltage is essentially the dc
component of the switch node <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.
The duty ratio, a timing quantity, now sets a dc voltage.

### The small-ripple approximation

A real filter leaves some switching ripple on the output. With a
well-designed filter the ripple is small compared with the dc value, and in
steady-state analysis the output voltage may be replaced by its dc value $V$:
the **small-ripple approximation** <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.
With it, the voltage across the inductor is constant within each
subinterval, so the inductor current rises and falls along straight lines;
the slope of those lines sets the current ripple. Apply the approximation to
quantities that are nearly constant, never to the ripple you are trying to
compute.

## Bench exercise

No power stage is needed. Feed a logic-level square wave from a function
generator into an R-C low-pass filter whose corner frequency is far below
the square wave's frequency. Measure the filter
output with a multimeter on its dc range while you change the duty cycle,
and compare each reading with the high level of the square wave times the
duty ratio. Then look at the same node with an oscilloscope: the remaining
ripple shrinks when you raise the frequency or the filter's time constant,
while the dc reading does not change.

## Gotchas

- **The average follows the switch node, not the gate signal.** Diode
  forward drops, switch resistance and dead time change the switch-node
  voltage during parts of the period, so the dc output departs from the ideal
  value set by the commanded duty ratio. The
  [averaged-models page](../averaged-models/) adds such losses to the model <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 3" />.
- **In DCM the switch node does not return to zero for the whole
  off-interval.** Once the inductor current has fallen to zero, the diode
  stops conducting and the switch node rings around the output voltage, so
  the output ends up above $D V_g$; see [CCM and DCM](../ccm-dcm/) <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 5" />.
