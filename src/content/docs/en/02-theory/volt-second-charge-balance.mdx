## Intent

- State inductor volt-second balance and capacitor charge balance, and why they hold in steady state.
- Use them to find the dc conversion ratio of a converter in continuous conduction mode (CCM).
- Recognise when the balance conditions do not apply.

## Theory

### Volt-second balance

In periodic steady state every waveform repeats from one switching period to
the next, so the inductor current ends each period where it began. The
inductor voltage integrated over one period equals $L$ times that net change
in current, which is zero: the average inductor voltage over a period is
zero, $\langle v_L \rangle = 0$ <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.
This is **inductor volt-second balance**.

<Figure name="balance" />

With the [small-ripple approximation](../switching-principle/), the inductor
voltage takes one constant value during the on-interval $D T_s$ and another
during the off-interval $(1 - D) T_s$. Balance fixes the second in terms of
the first:

<Eq id="vsb.v_off" />

The positive volt-seconds applied while the switch is on must be cancelled by
negative volt-seconds while it is off.

### When the average is not zero

Over one period, the inductor current changes by the average inductor
voltage times the period, over the inductance:

<Eq id="vsb.drift" />

During a transient (a start-up, a load or line step) this change carries
the converter towards its new steady state, period by period. A circuit in
which the average cannot reach zero has no steady state at all: a fixed
output fed in CCM at any duty ratio but the balancing one gains or loses the
same current every period, without bound <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.

### Charge balance

The same argument applies to a capacitor: its voltage ends each period where
it began, so the net charge delivered to it is zero and the average capacitor
current is zero, $\langle i_C \rangle = 0$. This is **capacitor charge
balance** <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />. In two-interval form:

<Eq id="csb.i_off" />

Volt-second balance gives the voltage relations of a converter; charge
balance gives its dc currents.

### Buck

While the switch is on, the inductor sits between $V_g$ and the output, so
its voltage is $V_g - V$. While the diode conducts, the inductor's input end
is grounded and its voltage is $-V$. Balancing the two gives the buck
conversion ratio <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />:

The boost places the inductor at the input. With the switch on, the inductor
sees $V_g$; with the diode conducting, it sees $V_g - V$. For these to
balance, $V$ must exceed $V_g$:

### Buck-boost

The buck-boost connects the inductor to $V_g$ while the switch is on, and
to the output while the diode conducts. Its output is inverted, so the
ratio is negative; its magnitude is below 1 for $D < 0.5$ and above 1 for
$D > 0.5$:

## Bench exercise

On a low-voltage converter evaluation board, measure the inductor voltage
with a differential probe (or two probes and the oscilloscope's math
subtraction). In steady state, read the two plateau voltages and the on and
off times, and check that the two volt-second areas are equal and opposite.
Then step the load and look again during the transient: while the inductor
current is changing, the areas are no longer equal.

## Gotchas

- **Balance holds only in steady state.** During start-up, load steps and
  line steps the average inductor voltage is not zero; that non-zero average
  is exactly what changes the inductor current <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.
- **Do not apply the small-ripple approximation to the ripple you are
  computing, or to the waveform that causes it.** The buck's output-voltage
  ripple is caused by the inductor current ripple, so the inductor current
  cannot be treated as constant when that ripple is computed <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />.
- **Losses change the balance equations.** Winding resistance and
  semiconductor drops add terms to the interval voltages and lower the ratio;
  see the [averaged-models page](../averaged-models/).
