## Intent

- Find the buck converter's dc inductor current, current ripple and peak current at an operating point.
- Estimate the output voltage ripple, and know what the ideal formula leaves out.
- State the voltage the switch and the diode must block, and where the buck leaves CCM.

## Theory

### Circuit and conversion ratio

The buck converter connects a switch between the input and the switch node,
a diode from the switch node to ground, and an L-C low-pass filter from the
switch node to the load <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />. The filter
passes the average of the switch node, so in CCM the conversion ratio equals
the duty ratio (<EqRef id="buck.ccm.M" label="buck conversion ratio" />).

### Output voltage ripple

The load takes the dc part of the inductor current and the capacitor takes
the triangular ripple. During the half-period in which that ripple is
positive, the capacitor gains a charge that raises its voltage from its
minimum to its maximum <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />:

<Eq id="buck.ripple.v" />

The capacitor is fed by a continuous triangular current rather than by
pulses, which keeps the buck's output ripple small for a given capacitance.
The derivation is on the [derivations page](../../02-theory/derivations/#deriv-topologies).

The switch blocks the input voltage while it is off, and the diode blocks it

Sweep $C$ over two decades (logarithmic axis): the ripple falls in inverse
proportion to the capacitance. Then halve $T_s$: at the same current ripple
the voltage ripple halves. In a real converter the current ripple halves
too, so doubling the switching frequency divides the output ripple by four.

## Bench exercise

On a low-voltage buck evaluation board, measure the inductor current with a
current probe and read its peak-to-peak ripple; compare it with the
peak-to-peak formula at the measured input voltage, output voltage and
switching frequency. Then measure the output ripple across the capacitor
terminals with a short probe ground connection. Expect more ripple than the
ideal formula predicts: the capacitor's series resistance and inductance
add their own terms.

## Gotchas

- **The capacitor's series resistance adds ripple.** It adds a voltage equal
  to that resistance times the peak-to-peak ripple current, in phase with the
  current. Compare it with the capacitive term above: depending on the
  capacitor, either can dominate.
- **Size the inductor for the peak current, not the dc current.** Above its
  saturation flux density a magnetic core loses its permeability and the
  inductance collapses <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />; the peak
  inductor current must stay below that limit.
- **The ripple formula assumes that the capacitor takes all the ripple.** That
  holds when the capacitor's impedance at the switching frequency is much
  lower than the load's.
