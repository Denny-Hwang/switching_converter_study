## Intent

- Choose between the basic converters by conversion range, isolation and control difficulty.
- Compare how hard each converter works its transistor, with one number: the transistor utilization.
- Know which converters have a right-half-plane zero and which draw pulsed input or output current.

## Theory

### Conversion range and isolation

The buck only steps down and the boost only steps up
(<EqRef id="buck.ccm.M" label="buck" />, <EqRef id="boost.ccm.M" label="boost" />).
The buck-boost does both, with an inverted output
(<EqRef id="buckboost.ccm.M" label="buck-boost" />). The flyback and the forward
converter add a transformer: the output is isolated and the turns ratio
scales the range (<EqRef id="flyback.ccm.M" label="flyback" />,
<EqRef id="forward.ccm.M" label="forward" />) <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 6" />.

### Transistor stress and utilization

A converter that needs a transistor rated for a high voltage and a high rms
current to deliver a given power uses that transistor poorly. One figure of
merit is the **transistor utilization**: the load power divided by the
product of the transistor's peak voltage and its rms current
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 6" />. With small ripple the transistor
current is a flat pulse lasting $D T_s$, and the utilization depends on the
duty ratio alone:

The buck approaches full utilization at large duty ratios. The boost is best
at small duty ratios, where it steps up only a little. The buck-boost and the
flyback are poorest, because the transistor blocks the sum of input and
reflected output voltage; their utilization peaks at $D = 1/3$. A forward
converter with $n_r = 1$ is limited to $D \le 0.5$ and blocks twice the input
voltage, which caps its utilization too. The derivations are on the
[derivations page](../../02-theory/derivations/#deriv-topologies).

### Control and current waveforms

The buck and the forward converter feed the output through an L-C filter
and have no right-half-plane zero. The boost, the buck-boost and the flyback
deliver energy to the output only during the off-interval, which creates the
[right-half-plane zero](../../02-theory/rhp-zero/) that limits their loop
bandwidth. The buck draws a pulsed input current and delivers a continuous
output current; the boost does the opposite; the buck-boost and the flyback
pulse at both ports <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 6" />.

## Bench exercise

With a current probe and an oscilloscope that computes rms values, measure
the transistor current and the drain-source voltage of two evaluation
boards of different topologies delivering similar power. Compute each
transistor's utilization from the peak voltage, the rms current and the
measured output power, and compare the results with the equations above at
the measured duty ratios.

## Gotchas

- **Utilization compares transistors only.** It leaves out diode stress,
  magnetics size, switching loss and the capacitors' ripple currents; use it
  as one criterion among several.
- **The small-ripple assumption flatters converters with large ripple.**
  A large current ripple raises the rms current above the flat-pulse value
  and lowers the real utilization.
- **Isolation is a requirement, not a figure of merit.** If the application
  needs galvanic isolation, only the transformer-based topologies qualify,
  whatever their utilization.
