## Intent

- Explain the skin effect in one conductor and the proximity effect between the layers of a winding.
- Estimate a layered winding's ac resistance with Dowell's factor, and for thin layers with its series.
- Choose the layer thickness and the number of layers, knowing that a thicker layer can lose more.

## Theory

### Skin effect

An alternating current crowds towards the surface of a conductor: its
density falls by a factor $e$ for every skin depth
(<EqRef id="wind.skin_depth" label="skin depth" />) into the conductor
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />. A conductor much thicker than a
skin depth carries the current in a surface layer, and its resistance is
higher than at dc.

### Proximity effect

In a winding of several layers, each layer sits in the field of the layers
beside it, which induces eddy currents in it. Their loss adds to that of the
layer's own current, and it grows quickly with the number of layers
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />.

There $F_R = 4/3$, whatever the number of layers. The more layers, the
thinner the best layer. Hurley, Gath and Breslin give the optimum for any
periodic current, from the rms values of the current and of its derivative
<Cite key="hurley2000" />.

### Converter currents

A converter's winding current is not sinusoidal. Its dc part sees the dc
resistance, and each harmonic sees Dowell's factor at its own frequency; the
loss is the sum over the harmonics <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />.
An inductor with a small ripple carries mostly dc, so $F_R$ matters little.
A flyback winding carries pulses, whose harmonics are large.

### Interleaving

Splitting a transformer's primary and placing the secondary between the
halves lowers the peak MMF in the window and the number of layers from zero
to peak MMF, and with them the proximity loss
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />. It also lowers the leakage
(see [leakage inductance](../leakage/)).

## Bench exercise

Measure the series resistance of a transformer's primary with its secondary
shorted, from a low frequency to a few times the switching frequency, and
well below the self-resonance (see [Measuring magnetics](../measurement/)).
The secondary's current then opposes the primary's, as in operation. Divide
by the dc resistance and compare the rise with Dowell's factor for your
number of layers.

## Gotchas

- **More copper can lose more.** Past the thickness of least loss, a thicker
  foil or a thicker wire raises the loss at the switching frequency.
- **Count the layers from zero MMF.** $M_\ell$ is the number of layers from a
  point of zero MMF to the peak; interleaving halves it
  <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />.
- **Next to a gap the field is not one-dimensional.** Dowell's factor assumes
  a one-dimensional field in the window
  (<EqRef id="wind.dowell" label="Dowell's factor" />), but the flux fringes
  around a gap <Cite key="dixon2001" />. Its loss estimate does not hold for
  turns close to a gap.
