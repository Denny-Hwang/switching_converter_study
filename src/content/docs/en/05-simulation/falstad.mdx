## Intent

- Open the SPICE library's circuits in CircuitJS1 and read their scopes.
- Know how CircuitJS1 steps a circuit, and what can happen within a step where a diode stops conducting.
- Tell a numerical spike from the circuit's own, and know why the library's circuits carry body diodes, backward Euler magnetics and a coupling just below one.

## Theory

### The library in CircuitJS1

CircuitJS1 is Paul Falstad's circuit simulator, adapted to the browser by
Iain Sharp <Cite key="falstad_circuitjs" />: it runs a circuit in the page and
animates the currents through it. The repository's `sim/falstad/` folder has
the SPICE library's seven cases as CircuitJS1 circuits, each as a text file
(File > Import From Text) and as a link that opens it on falstad.com. The
link carries the whole circuit, compressed into its address
<Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />. A script writes every circuit from the list
of parts behind the SPICE netlist of the same name, with the same example's
values, and checks that each part joins the same nodes as in the netlist.

<FalstadLinks />

The scopes along the bottom show the inductor current (in the flyback, the
switch current), the switch node or the drain, and the output. A circuit opens
at about one switching period a second, with current dots slow enough to
follow; the Simulation Speed slider runs it faster. It starts from the ideal
steady state's values, the output capacitor at the output voltage and the
inductor (the flyback's magnetizing inductance) at its current when the switch
turns on, so the scopes show the steady state within the first periods. Reset
returns the capacitor and inductor to these values.

The site's CI opens a copy of every link on falstad.com in a headless browser,
the same circuit with only its speed raised. It runs the copy for as many
periods as the SPICE case and compares the averages and extremes of its last
two periods with the ideal values in the table: each must lie within one
percent of its quantity's largest magnitude over those periods.

### How CircuitJS1 steps a circuit

CircuitJS1 advances the whole circuit by one fixed time step at a time. It
halves the step only when its iteration fails to converge, and then only if
Auto-Adjust Timestep is on in Other Options <Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />.
The library's circuits leave it off and step a five-hundredth of the
switching period. SPICE instead chooses each step from an estimate of its
error <Cite key="ngspice_manual" />; CircuitJS1 does not look for the instant
a diode starts or stops conducting.

In every step each inductor becomes a companion model, a conductance in
parallel with a current source. The model follows the trapezoidal rule
unless the inductor's Trapezoidal Approximation box is unchecked, which
selects backward Euler, and a transformer's windings work the same way
<Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />. A diode is nonlinear, so CircuitJS1
iterates: it replaces the diode by a straight line at its present voltage,
solves the circuit, and repeats. It counts the diode as converged once its
voltage changes by no more than 10 mV from one iteration to the next <Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />,
and then keeps the solution it has without solving again.

### Where a diode stops conducting

The library's diodes are near ideal: a change of their voltage far smaller
than what the iteration accepts changes their current many times over. When
a diode's current reaches zero within a step, the straight line that stands
for the diode can still carry current past zero, for that step and perhaps
the next, and the inductor's current ends below zero. In the library's DCM
buck it goes past zero by a little more than it changes in one step. Once the
iteration finds the diode off, and nothing else takes the current, the next
step has to bring the current back to zero, and backward Euler puts across
the inductor, for that one step:

<Eq id="sim.step_jump" />

The node the current leaves ends up that far from the inductor's other end:
in the DCM buck without its body diode, the switch node leaps for one step
from near zero to that much above the output, about ten volts above the
input, a spike the circuit does not have. A shorter step does not remove it.
The current's overshoot is about its change in one step, so it shrinks with
the step, and the jump stays about as large as the voltage the inductor had
across it before the diode turned off, the output voltage in the buck: with
the step halved and halved again, the DCM buck's jump stayed between about
0.7 and 1.4 times its output voltage.

The library's circuits therefore give each switch a body diode, as the
site's simulator has. It bounds the jump without removing it: the current
past zero flows through it, so a node goes no further than a diode drop past
a rail. The DCM buck's switch node stops a diode drop above the input for a
few steps, then dips below the output for one step as the body diode's own
current goes past zero. The DCM flyback's drain, which falls back to the
input when the diode turns off, can dip for a step, but no further than a
diode drop below ground, where without the body diode it reaches far below.
A capacitance at the node, stepped by backward Euler, takes the current
without a jump.

### Transformers

CircuitJS1's transformer inverts the matrix of its windings' inductances,
which is singular when the coupling is exactly one, and its dialog accepts a
coupling only between zero and one <Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />. The
library's transformers use 0.99999. The leakage inductance left adds a spike
to the drain for one step at turn-off, as the switch stops its current within
that step (<EqRef id="sim.step_jump" label="one-step jump" />, with the
leakage inductance the switch's current flows through and the current it
interrupts). That current does
not shrink with the step, so the spike, less than a volt at the library's
step, is proportional to one minus the coupling and to one over the step.

That leakage is also a mode far faster than the step, and the trapezoidal
rule's factor for such a mode is close to minus one
(<EqRef id="num.gain_tr" label="the trapezoidal rule's factor" />): it rings
from one step to the next. With the Trapezoidal Approximation box ticked,
while the output diode conducts, the DCM flyback's drain alternates at every
step between about twice the level it should show and zero. The library's
inductors and transformers therefore use
backward Euler, whose factor for such a mode is close to zero
(<EqRef id="num.gain_be" label="backward Euler's factor" />).

## Worked example

The library's DCM buck steps its inductor with backward Euler. Take its body
diode away, and the current past zero when the freewheeling diode turns off
has to stop within one step:

The switch node jumps <Val example="falstad-jump" name="v_jump" /> above the
output, which sits at <Val example="buck-light-load" name="V_dcm" />, so for
one step it shows about ten volts above the input. With the body diode in
place, the current flows back to the input through it, and the switch node
stops a diode drop above the input for a few steps.

see it in CircuitJS1: open the DCM buck from the table, select the diode
across the switch and delete it, and watch the switch node's scope spike at

spike stays. In the DCM flyback, double-click the transformer and tick

## Bench exercise

Capture a DCM converter's switch node on the bench as the diode stops
conducting: the inductance rings with the capacitance at the switch node
(<EqRef id="dcm.ring.f" label="ringing frequency" />). Work out that
capacitance from the ring's frequency and the inductance. In CircuitJS1, give
the library's DCM buck your converter's values, add the capacitance from the
switch node to ground with its Trapezoidal Approximation box unchecked, and
set a time step short enough to resolve the ring, a small fraction of its
period. The switch node now leaves zero smoothly and rings at the bench's
frequency, clipped by the body diode at the input if it swings that far.
Compare the frequency, not the decay: backward Euler damps an oscillation by
itself, as its factor for an oscillation is below one in size
<Cite key="hairer1996" where="Sec. IV.3" />, and the fewer steps a ring
period spans, the sooner the simulated ring dies out.

## Gotchas

- **A spike that lasts one step, and changes when the step changes,** is
  numerical: look for a current with nowhere to go
  (<EqRef id="sim.step_jump" label="one-step jump" />) before you look for a
  cause in the circuit.
- **A node that alternates at every step** is the trapezoidal rule on a mode
  far faster than the step. Uncheck Trapezoidal Approximation on the
  inductors, transformers and capacitors in that mode.
- **A coupling of exactly one cannot be entered**: the dialog refuses it
  <Cite key="circuitjs1_source" />. Use a value just below one, and expect a
  small leakage spike at turn-off.
- **A diode with a steep curve converges loosely.** The iteration stops on a
  change of voltage, not of current, so a near-ideal diode can end a step with
  the inductor's current well past zero. Give the current a path: a body diode
  bounds the jump, and a node capacitance stepped by backward Euler
  (Trapezoidal Approximation unchecked) removes it.
