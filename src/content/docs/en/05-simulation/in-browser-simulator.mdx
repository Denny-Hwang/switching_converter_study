## Intent

- See why a switching converter is a linear circuit within each switching interval, and what stepping it exactly means.
- Compare the exact step with a simple integration rule on a fast time constant.
- Know how the simulator finds the periodic steady state, and what that saves over a run from rest.

## Theory

### A linear circuit in each interval

Between two switching events, every switch and diode of the
[simulator](../../simulate/simulator/) is either on or off, and what is left
is a linear circuit: resistances, inductances, capacitances and constant
sources. Its state, the inductor currents and the capacitor voltages, obeys
linear differential equations with constant sources in each interval, the
same description that state-space averaging starts from
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 7" />. For a capacitor charged through a
resistance, the solution over a step is exact, however long the step:

<Eq id="sim.exact_step" />

A circuit with several states steps them all at once with the exponential of
its equations' matrix, which the simulator computes by scaling and squaring
<Cite key="higham2005" />. Within an interval nothing is approximated beyond
rounding, so the length of a step does not limit its accuracy.

### A simple rule on a fast time constant

A general-purpose integrator approximates the solution over small steps
instead. The simplest rule, forward Euler, steps with the slope at the start
of the step, and multiplies a decaying mode's deviation by a fixed factor in
every step <Cite key="hairer1996" />:

<Eq id="num.gain_fe" />

A step longer than twice the time constant makes the factor larger than one
in size, and the solution grows without bound. A converter has fast time
constants, such as a small capacitance at the switch node behind a small
resistance, next to the slow ones of its output filter: a rule like this
would have to take steps shorter than the fastest, over as many periods as
the slowest needs. SPICE avoids the blow-up with implicit rules, which keep a
decaying mode from growing whatever the step (see the
[ngspice page](../ngspice/)).

### Events

An interval ends when a switch changes state: at the gate's edges, and when a
diode's current reaches zero or its voltage turns it on. The simulator finds
that instant within the step on the same exact solution, so the next
interval starts from the right state
([how it computes](../../simulate/simulator/#how-it-computes)).

### The periodic steady state

From rest, a converter's output settles with its filter's envelope time
constant (<EqRef id="filter.tau_env" label="envelope time constant" />), and
reaching a small fraction of the start-up error takes many periods
(<EqRef id="sim.periods_settle" label="periods to settle" />). The simulator
looks instead for the state at the start of a period that the period maps
back onto itself: the periodic steady state, a fixed point of the map from
one period's initial state to the next. Newton's method finds it with the
map's derivative, the product of the steps' matrices, in a few iterations
<Cite key="aprille1972" />. When the switching instants do not depend on the
state, as in CCM with a fixed duty ratio, the map is linear and a single
Newton step lands on the steady state.

## Bench exercise

Charge a capacitor through a resistor from a function generator's square
wave, and capture the capacitor's voltage. Read it at the start of a
half-period and one time constant later, and compare with the exact step.
Then take the samples twice the time constant apart and apply forward
Euler's rule by hand from the first one: the first step already overshoots
the final voltage, while the circuit does not.

## Gotchas

- **The steady state is not the start-up.** The simulator reports the
  periodic steady state; a start-up or a load step on the bench passes
  through transients it does not show.
- **A small step does not make an explicit rule stable by itself.** It must
  be shorter than twice the *fastest* time constant, even when that mode is
  too fast to matter (<EqRef id="num.gain_fe" label="forward Euler's factor" />).
- **The plots draw samples.** A peak narrower than a sub-step can be in the
  reported numbers but not in the plot.
