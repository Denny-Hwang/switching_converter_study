## Intent

- Open and run the SPICE library's LTspice schematics, and plot what their READMEs list.
- Work out how long a simulation from rest must run before its last periods show the steady state.
- Know which directives the schematics carry, and why.

## Theory

### The library in LTspice

The repository's `sim/ltspice/` folder has the five basic converters as
LTspice schematics <Cite key="ltspice" />, one folder per converter with a
README that says what to plot and what to expect. Each schematic is the same
circuit as the ngspice netlist of the same name in `sim/ngspice/`: the
library's script reads every schematic back into a netlist and compares it
part by part and node by node, and the nets carry the netlist's node names.
Open a schematic and press Run: its directives come with it.

- `.param` sets the values of the synthetic example that the simulator's
  preset of the same name uses.
- `.model` gives the near-ideal switch and diode.
- `.tran` runs from rest, with every capacitor empty and every inductor
  without current, for 400 to 2500 periods depending on the case, with a
  time step no longer than a two-hundredth of a period.
- `.options method=gear reltol=1e-4` chooses the integration method and the
  tolerance. The [ngspice page](../ngspice/) explains why.

The ngspice netlists also carry `.meas` statements that average each quantity
and find its extremes over the last period. In LTspice, zoom in on the last
period, or add the same statements to the schematic.

### How long to run

A run from rest has to pass through the whole start-up before its last period
shows the steady state. While the converter stays in CCM, its output filter
and the load ring after the start, and with ideal parts the ring's envelope
decays with a time constant set by the load and the capacitance alone:

<Eq id="filter.tau_env" />

The error left after the start-up shrinks by the same factor in every
envelope time constant. To bring it down to a fraction of what it was at
the start takes:

<Eq id="sim.periods_settle" />

A light load rings longer, and each factor of ten in accuracy adds the same
number of periods. Real parts damp the ring a little more: the winding's
resistance, the capacitor's series resistance and the switch's
on-resistance. A start-up can also leave CCM for a while: in the library's
buck, the inductor current falls to zero in several of its first twenty
periods, after the output's first overshoot. The formula is then an
estimate, not an exact count. In DCM the inductor current returns to zero in
every period, so the inductor carries nothing from one period to the next:
the filter no longer rings as an L-C pair, and near its steady state the
output settles with a single time constant instead
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15" />.

The library runs each case for many times its slowest time constant, and its
script checks each run at the end: the output's average over the last period
must lie within one part in ten thousand of its average over the period
before, or the case fails as not settled. The check only catches a run far
too short. In one period a decaying error shrinks only by a fraction of
itself about equal to the period over the time constant, so with an envelope
time constant of a hundred periods, as in the library's CCM flyback and
forward cases, a change of one part in ten thousand still allows an error of
about a percent; and near a crest of the ring, where the output's average
stops rising and starts to fall, two consecutive averages can agree while
the ring is still large.
SPICE's own accuracy sets a floor, too: in the library's ngspice runs, the
average shifts by up to a few parts in ten thousand long after the start-up
has died away.

### Integration settings

With LTspice's default settings the library's DCM flyback shows a dip of the
drain voltage tens of volts below zero that the circuit does not have: about
as deep as the one ngspice draws with Gear's method at its default
tolerance, not the kilovolt of ngspice's trapezoidal ringing. The `.options`
line removes it, as it does in ngspice. The library records a one-time check
with LTspice 26.1.1, in which LTspice's own netlists matched the library's
and its results agreed with ngspice's within 0.3 % of each quantity's
largest magnitude. The [ngspice page](../ngspice/) explains both effects:
the factor by which each step multiplies a fast mode
(<EqRef id="num.gain_tr" label="the trapezoidal rule's factor" />), and a
diode's current overshooting through zero at a loose tolerance.

## Bench exercise

Start a buck at a fixed duty ratio, without a feedback loop, into a
resistor, and capture its output from the moment it starts switching.
Measure the envelope's decay from two peaks of the ring a known time apart,
after the inductor current has stopped touching zero if it does in the first
periods, and compare the time constant with twice the load resistance times
the output capacitance: the winding's resistance, the capacitor's series
resistance and the switch's on-resistance damp the ring further, so expect a
somewhat shorter one. Count the periods until the output stays within one
percent of its final value, and compare with the formula for one percent
left. Then run the library's schematic with your values and the same start.

## Gotchas

- **A measurement over a period that has not settled** measures the start-up.
  Run for the periods the formula asks for, then check that the output's
  average no longer changes from one period to the next, as the library
  does: a small change alone does not prove that a slow ring has settled.
- **The envelope formula holds in CCM, with a ringing filter, ideal parts and
  no feedback loop.** In DCM, with a filter damped so heavily that it does
  not ring, or under closed-loop control, the output settles another way
  (<EqRef id="filter.tau_env" label="envelope time constant" />).
- **A spike that the circuit cannot make** is often numerical: check the
  `.options` line before you look for a cause in the circuit.
