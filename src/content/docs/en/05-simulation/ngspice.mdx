## Intent

- Run a library netlist in batch mode and read its measurements.
- See what an integration rule does to a mode much faster than its time step, and why the trapezoidal rule can ring where the circuit does not.
- Choose the options that make a switching waveform trustworthy.

## Theory

### Running a netlist

The repository's `sim/ngspice/` folder has each converter case as a netlist
for ngspice <Cite key="ngspice" />. Run `ngspice -b buck-ccm.cir` and it
prints the `.meas` results of the last period: averages, largest and smallest
values of the switch voltage, the inductor current and the output. Run
`ngspice buck-ccm.cir`, then `run`, to plot what the folder's README lists.
CI runs every netlist again and requires each value within one percent of the
ideal equations, and the output settled.

### How SPICE steps a circuit

SPICE computes the circuit at a sequence of time points. It chooses each step
so that the error it estimates for its integration rule stays within its
tolerances, and the relative tolerance is 0.001 unless the netlist sets
another <Cite key="ngspice_manual" />. Between two points, every capacitor and
inductor follows the rule that `.options method` selects: trapezoidal unless
told otherwise, or Gear's <Cite key="ngspice_manual" />.

### What the rule does to a fast mode

A converter's circuit can have modes far faster than the time step, for
example where a diode stops conducting. Each rule multiplies such a mode's
deviation by a fixed factor per step. The trapezoidal rule averages the slopes at both
ends of the step:

<Eq id="num.gain_tr" />

Its factor stays within one in size for any step, so the solution does not
blow up, but for a step much longer than the mode's time constant it
approaches minus one: the mode barely decays and flips its sign at every time
point, a ringing that the circuit does not have <Cite key="hairer1996" />.
Backward Euler, the first of Gear's methods, takes the slope at the end of
the step:

<Eq id="num.gain_be" />

Its factor tends to zero instead, so such a mode dies out within the step
<Cite key="hairer1996" />.

### What the library found

With ngspice's defaults, the trapezoidal rule and a relative tolerance of
0.001, the library's DCM flyback shows exactly this: once the diode stops
conducting, the secondary current rings through zero from one step to the
next, and the drain voltage swings to about a kilovolt below zero. Gear's
method at the same tolerance removes the ringing, but the diode's current
still overshoots through zero as its conduction ends, and the drain dips tens
of volts below zero for that instant. With `.options method=gear reltol=1e-4`,
the line every netlist and schematic in the library carries, both are gone.

## Bench exercise

Capture a DCM converter's switch node on the bench as the diode stops
conducting. The ring there is real: the inductance rings with the capacitance
at the switch node, at a frequency those parts set. Simulate the same circuit
twice, with the trapezoidal rule and a long maximum step, and with Gear's
method. A ring that alternates at every time point, and moves when you change
the maximum step, is numerical; one that keeps its frequency is the
circuit's, and it should match the bench.

## Gotchas

- **A ring that flips sign at every time point** belongs to the integration
  rule, not to the circuit (<EqRef id="num.gain_tr" label="the trapezoidal rule's factor" />).
- **Gear's methods also damp real rings they do not resolve.** For an
  undamped oscillation, backward Euler's factor is below one in size and the
  trapezoidal rule's is exactly one <Cite key="hairer1996" />: a lightly
  damped L-C ring decays faster in a Gear simulation than on the bench unless
  the step is short compared with its period.
- **A tighter tolerance is not free.** It shortens the steps and lengthens the
  run. The library chose it for the DCM flyback's diode turn-off and applies it
  to every case alike.
