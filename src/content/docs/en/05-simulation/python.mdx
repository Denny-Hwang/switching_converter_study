## Intent

- Follow one equation from its SymPy derivation to the page and to the tests.
- Compare two computations by their relative difference, and choose a tolerance that rounding cannot fail and a real mistake cannot pass.
- Run the site's checks on your own machine.

## Theory

### One source, two implementations

Every equation on the site lives once, in
`packages/pe-core/equations/equations.yaml`, as a SymPy expression with its
symbols, its tests and its citation. The Python package `python/pe_core`
works from it:

- Where it can, a script in `python/pe_core/derive/` derives the equation from
  first principles, solving the circuit's balance equations with SymPy step by
  step. The [derivations page](../../02-theory/derivations/) shows those steps
  as the code computes them, and pytest checks that each result equals the
  catalogue's expression.
- `python scripts/gen_equations.py` writes the LaTeX the pages show and the
  test vectors: each equation's own tests, and random inputs evaluated by
  SymPy. CI runs it again and fails on any difference.

The TypeScript engine behind the pages and the tools has its own evaluators,
written by hand rather than generated from the same file, so that a mistake
in one is not copied into the other. vitest checks every evaluator against
the vectors.

### Comparing two numbers

Two computations are compared by their relative difference:

<Eq id="num.rel_diff" />

It does not depend on the unit or the scale, so one tolerance serves every
quantity; near a zero reference it does not work, and an absolute limit is
needed there <Cite key="higham2002" />. Two correct programs need not produce
the same bits: each operation rounds its result, and the result depends on
the order of the operations. One rounding changes a number by at most the
unit roundoff of the format, relative to its size:

<Eq id="num.unit_roundoff" />

The site requires the TypeScript and the Python evaluations of an equation to
agree within one part in a billion. That is millions of times the unit
roundoff, so rounding passes, yet far below any real mistake such as a wrong
constant or a missing factor. A simulation compared with its equation gets a
looser tolerance, because the circuit it simulates is not exactly the ideal
one of the equation: the SPICE library's cases must lie within one percent of
the ideal equations, and the in-browser simulator within two percent.

### Checks against another solver

`scripts/spice_crosscheck.py` writes the in-browser simulator's circuits
again as netlists, from their schematics rather than from the simulator's
equations, and runs them in ngspice <Cite key="ngspice" />. CI compares the
simulator with ngspice's results within half a percent.

## Bench exercise

Measure a converter's input and output voltages on the bench with a
multimeter, compute the relative difference of their ratio from the ideal
one in Python, and list what can account for it: the diode's drop, the
switch's and the winding's resistances, the ripple. Then change the load and
see which of them grows with the current.

## Gotchas

- **A reference near zero** makes the relative difference meaningless; compare
  against a scale, such as the quantity's largest value over the period, as
  the library's checks do.
- **Two implementations that share code share its mistakes.** Generated
  evaluators would agree with the file they come from even where it is wrong.
- **Floating-point numbers are not compared for equality.** 0.1 + 0.2 is not
  0.3 in binary64: compare within a tolerance
  (<EqRef id="num.unit_roundoff" label="unit roundoff" />).
