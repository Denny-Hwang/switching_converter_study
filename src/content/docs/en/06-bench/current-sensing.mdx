## Intent

- Turn a current into a voltage with a shunt, and know what it costs the circuit.
- Choose between the low side and the high side for the shunt.
- Keep the pads and the solder out of the reading with a Kelvin connection.

## Theory

### The shunt

By Ohm's law a shunt turns the current into a sense voltage
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 2" />:

<Eq id="sense.burden" />

That voltage is the amplifier's signal, and also the burden: the voltage
the shunt takes from the circuit it measures. A larger shunt gives a larger
signal and a smaller share of error from the amplifier's offset
<Cite key="ti_ina181" />, at the price of more burden and more dissipation
(<EqRef id="loss.cond" label="conduction loss" />).

### Low side or high side

On the **low side** the shunt sits in the ground return, so the load's
ground rises above the supply's by the sense voltage. Low-side sensing suits
cost-sensitive designs that can tolerate that ground disturbance, and
high-side sensing is preferable when it cannot be tolerated
<Cite key="ti_sszta51" />. On the **high side** both of the shunt's
terminals sit near the supply voltage, so the amplifier must accept that
voltage at its inputs: current-sense amplifiers are specified for low-side
use, high-side use or both <Cite key="ti_ina181" /> <Cite key="adi_ltc6101" />.

### Pads, solder and the Kelvin connection

With a shunt of a few milliohms or less, the resistance of the solder
becomes a substantial part of the sense element's resistance, and adds to
the measurement error <Cite key="osullivan2012" />. If the sense connections
sit beyond the pads, the amplifier sees the current through the shunt and
the pads together, and the reading is high by the same fraction at every
current:

<Eq id="sense.pad_error" />

A Kelvin connection takes the pads out of the sense path: a four-terminal
shunt, or a two-terminal one on a four-pad (split-pad) layout
<Cite key="osullivan2012" />. The LTC6101 data sheet recommends Kelvin
connections to the sense resistor in all but the lowest-power applications
<Cite key="adi_ltc6101" where="Applications Information" />.

The amplifier, its offset, the output's range and the filter before an ADC
are worked through in the [sense chain](../../design/sense-chain/) tool.

## Bench exercise

Pass a known current through a low-value shunt on a board. Measure the
voltage across it twice: once at the ends of its pads, once at the shunt's
own terminals (or its Kelvin pads). Compare each with the current times the
shunt's rated value, and estimate the pads' resistance from the difference.
Then move the shunt from the ground return to the supply side and watch the
load's ground: it no longer moves with the current.

## Gotchas

- **A low-side shunt lifts the load's ground** by its sense voltage
  <Cite key="ti_sszta51" />: a signal the load sends to a ground-referenced
  circuit moves with the current.
- **Sensing beyond the pads** adds their resistance to the shunt's, a fixed
  fraction of every reading <Cite key="osullivan2012" />
  (<EqRef id="sense.pad_error" label="pad error" />).
- **A high-side shunt** puts the supply's voltage on the amplifier's
  inputs: use an amplifier specified for high-side sensing
  <Cite key="ti_ina181" />.
