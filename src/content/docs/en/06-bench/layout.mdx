## Intent

- Find the loop in a converter whose current switches on and off at every edge.
- Estimate the voltage that loop's inductance develops over an edge.
- Place the input capacitor, the ground layer, the switch node and the feedback trace accordingly.

## Theory

### The hot loop

In a buck converter the switch draws a pulse of the inductor's current from
the input capacitor while it is on; the source, behind its wiring, supplies
about the average and recharges the capacitor while the switch is off. At
every edge the currents of the switch, of the diode (or the second switch)
and of the input capacitor step by the inductor's current, while the
inductor's current flows on <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />: the
loop of those three is the one whose current switches. Every loop has
inductance, and over an edge that changes the current by $\Delta I$ in
$t_\mathrm{edge}$ it develops a voltage
(<EqRef id="ind.di" label="inductor law" />):

<Eq id="layout.v_spike" />

By Kirchhoff's voltage law that voltage adds to the input voltage across
the switch while its current falls <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 2 (Basic Laws)" />:
the overshoot at every turn-off edge. For the same edge it is proportional
to the loop's inductance, and a shorter, tighter loop has less. The input
capacitor closes that loop, so it goes right at the switches: TI calls it
the single most important component for the reliable operation of a
step-down converter <Cite key="ti_slyt614" />.

After the turn-off edge the loop's inductance rings with the switch node's
capacitance, and the ring's frequency gives the inductance without a
current probe in the loop:

<Eq id="layout.L_ring" />

### The ground layer and the switch node

A ground layer directly next to the outside layer that carries the power
stage belongs in every such board <Cite key="adi_an136" />. The switch node
swings between the input voltage and ground at every edge, and the stray
capacitance between its copper and a neighbouring trace carries a current
in proportion to that swing's rate (<EqRef id="cap.dv" label="capacitor law" />).
But the node also carries the inductor's current and draws heat from the
switches, so its area cannot be too small either: AN-136 treats it as a
compromise <Cite key="adi_an136" />.

### The feedback trace

The controller regulates what the feedback trace brings it: a voltage
picked up on the way is regulated into the output as if it were the
output's own. Route the trace as far from the inductor and the noisy power
traces as possible <Cite key="ti_snva021" />. Since the controller
regulates the voltage at the trace's far end, take it from the point where
the output voltage matters, with its own return (a Kelvin connection, see
[current sensing](../current-sensing/)).

## Bench exercise

On a converter board, trace the hot loop: the input capacitor, the switch
and the diode or second switch. Measure the overshoot at turn-off across
the low-side switch or diode, whose one end is at ground, with the shortest
ground connection your probe allows (see [probes](../probes/)); a switch
whose terminals both move needs a differential probe. A current probe needs
a wire loop added to the hot loop, which adds inductance of its own, so
estimate the loop's inductance from the ring that follows the edge instead,
with the whole capacitance at the switch node: the switch's and the diode's
output capacitances from their data sheets, plus the probe's input
capacitance and the board's. The ring measured again with a known capacitor
added gives all of it at once (as in
[snubbers and clamps](../../04-magnetics/snubbers-clamps/)). With the edge's
current step and fall time, compare the overshoot with the loop-voltage
equation.
Then add a small ceramic capacitor right across the switch pair, making the
loop shorter, and measure again.

## Gotchas

- **An input capacitor placed far away** makes the loop that switches its
  current large, and the overshoot with it
  (<EqRef id="layout.v_spike" label="loop voltage" />).
- **A switch node with too little copper** runs hot, and one with too much
  couples its swing into its neighbours: its area is a compromise
  <Cite key="adi_an136" />.
- **A long probe ground lead** adds its own loop and makes the overshoot
  look larger than it is <Cite key="adi_an1144" /> (see [probes](../probes/)).
