## Intent

- Estimate the peak current and the joule integral when a supply is switched onto an empty input capacitor.
- Choose a voltage ramp for a given inrush current, and know where its energy goes.
- Know what warm air does to a fuse, and what a series diode against a reversed battery costs.

## Theory

### Inrush from a step

At the instant a supply is switched onto an empty capacitor, the capacitor
has no voltage across it. When the wiring's inductance is small, its own
time constant (the inductance over the series resistance) far shorter than
$R_\mathrm{ser} C_\mathrm{in}$, the whole step falls across the resistance
in series with the capacitor: the source's, the wiring's and the capacitor's
own ESR. That resistance then sets the peak <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 7 (First-Order Circuits)" />:

<Eq id="inrush.I_pk" />

The current then decays with the time constant $R_\mathrm{ser} C_\mathrm{in}$.
The integral of its square over the pulse, its joule integral, is

<Eq id="inrush.I2t" />

A resistance in the path times the joule integral is the heat the pulse
leaves in it. The series resistance as a whole takes as much as the
capacitor stores, whatever its value
(<EqRef id="cap.E" label="energy stored in a capacitor" />), and each part
of it (a fuse's element, a switch, the wiring) takes its own share.

More inductance in the leads slows the current's rise and lowers its peak,
which then depends on the capacitance too; with little resistance, the
capacitor's voltage rings above the supply. The joule integral does not
change: the source still delivers twice what the capacitor stores, and the
resistance dissipates the rest.

### A controlled ramp

Making the voltage across the capacitor rise more slowly reduces the inrush
<Cite key="ti_slva670" />. A capacitor's current is its capacitance times
the rate of change of its voltage, so a linear ramp from zero draws a
constant current:

<Eq id="inrush.I_ramp" />

When an element in series with a fixed supply (a load switch or a hot-swap
switch) sets the ramp, it carries that current while it drops the rest of
the supply's voltage. Over the ramp, the path still dissipates as much as
the capacitor stores, and nearly all of it in that element when the ramp is
long against $R_\mathrm{ser} C_\mathrm{in}$. The ramp lowers the current and
spreads the energy over time; it does not remove it. A source that ramps
its own output, such as a converter's soft start, has no such element to
heat.

### A fuse in warm air

The higher the ambient temperature, the hotter a fuse operates, and the
shorter its life <Cite key="littelfuse_fuseology" />. A fuse beside a hot
heat sink or inside a warm enclosure runs in a higher ambient than the same
fuse on an open bench.

### A reversed battery

A diode in series with the battery protects against a battery connected
backwards <Cite key="ti_slva139" />: reversed, the diode blocks. The right way round, it carries the whole load current for as long
as the circuit runs, and costs its forward drop times the average current,
plus its resistance's share
(<EqRef id="loss.diode" label="diode conduction loss" />). The drop is a
larger fraction of a lower battery voltage, and so is the loss.

## Bench exercise

Start from a low voltage and a current-limited source. With a current probe
on the input lead, switch the source onto a converter's empty input
capacitor and capture the current's peak and its decay; a current limit in
the source caps the peak, so note it. Estimate the path's resistance: the
wiring's and the switch's with a four-wire milliohm meter, the capacitor's
ESR from its data sheet or an impedance meter at a high frequency, and the
source's from its data sheet or its voltage drop under a known load step.
Compare the peak with the step's equation: a lower peak and a capacitor
voltage ringing above the supply point to the leads' inductance. Square the
captured current in the oscilloscope's math, integrate it, and compare with
the joule integral.
Then slow the switch's turn-on and repeat: the peak falls. Capture the
switch's voltage as well; its product with the current, integrated over
the ramp, is the energy the switch now absorbs.

## Gotchas

- **In the R-C model the capacitance does not limit the peak** from a step;
  only the series resistance does
  (<EqRef id="inrush.I_pk" label="peak inrush current" />). Inductance in
  the leads lowers the peak and can make the capacitor's voltage ring above
  the supply.
- **A ramp set by a series element does not remove the energy.** The path
  still dissipates as much as the capacitor stores, now mostly in that
  element (<EqRef id="inrush.I_ramp" label="ramped inrush" />).
- **A warm fuse ages faster** <Cite key="littelfuse_fuseology" />.
- **A series diode costs power for as long as the circuit runs**, not only
  when something goes wrong (<EqRef id="loss.diode" label="diode conduction loss" />).
