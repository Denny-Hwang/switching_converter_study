## Intent

- Estimate a device's junction temperature from its power and the thermal resistances along the heat's path.
- Know what a data sheet's junction-to-ambient resistance does not tell about your board.
- Estimate the junction from a temperature measured on top of the package.

## Theory

### A series path

A thermal resistance is a temperature difference per watt of the power that
flows across it <Cite key="ti_spra953" />. On a heat sink, a device sheds
its power mainly from its junction to its case, through the interface (a
pad, grease or an insulator) to the heat sink, and from the heat sink to the
air. Taking all of it along that path, each part raises the temperature by
its resistance times the power:

<Eq id="therm.Tj" />

The largest resistance in the chain takes the largest share of the
junction's rise, so lowering it helps most.

A data sheet's junction-to-ambient resistance $\theta_\mathrm{JA}$ is the
most commonly reported thermal metric, and it is often misused
<Cite key="ti_spra953" />: it describes the whole path from the junction to
the air as the device was measured, board and airflow included, not the
path on your board.

### From the package top

A device without a heat sink sheds its power through its leads, its board
and its top, and only part of the power flows through the top. The
junction-to-top characterization parameter $\Psi_\mathrm{JT}$ relates the
junction to a temperature measured on top of the package, for the device's
whole power; it is not a true thermal resistance, and is not added into a
series path <Cite key="ti_spra953" />:

<Eq id="therm.psi" />

A data sheet's value holds in the conditions it was characterized in; with
a heat sink on the package it no longer applies.

### Temperature and loss

A silicon MOSFET's on-resistance rises with its junction temperature: in
the cited tutorial it roughly doubles from 25 °C to 125 °C <Cite key="microchip_apt0403" />,
and the data sheet's normalized on-resistance curve gives the factor for the
part you use. The conduction loss rises with it
(<EqRef id="loss.cond" label="conduction loss" />). A hotter junction
dissipates more, which heats it further: estimate the loss at the junction
temperature it causes, not at room temperature.

## Bench exercise

Run a converter at full load until its temperatures settle, and find each
device's power from the loss model. The measured input less output is the
whole converter's loss, every switch, diode, magnetic part and capacitor and
the control circuit together: it checks the loss model's total, not any one
device's power.
For a switch on a heat sink, measure the ambient and estimate the junction
through the series path. For a device without a heat sink (a controller, or
a surface-mount switch cooled by its board), measure the top of its package
and estimate the junction with the data sheet's characterization parameter:
set a thermal camera to the surface's emissivity, and keep a thermocouple's
wire from drawing heat off a small package. Compare each estimate with what
the data sheet's junction-to-ambient resistance would predict.

## Gotchas

- **The junction-to-ambient resistance** belongs to the board and airflow
  it was measured with, and is often misused <Cite key="ti_spra953" />.
- **A characterization parameter is not a thermal resistance**: do not add
  it into a series path <Cite key="ti_spra953" />.
- **Loss estimated at room temperature** is too low: the on-resistance
  rises with the junction's temperature <Cite key="microchip_apt0403" />.
