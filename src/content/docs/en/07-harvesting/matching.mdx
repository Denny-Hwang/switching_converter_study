## Intent

- Work out the share of a linear source's available power that a constant voltage and a loss-free resistor take.
- See why a converter between the source and a battery lets the source work at its best voltage.
- Know that a converter in DCM with a fixed duty ratio is a loss-free resistor, and what it does as the source's amplitude changes.

## Theory

### A constant voltage

A battery, or a bus held at a fixed voltage $V_c$, connected straight to a
linear source draws the current that the difference between the two
voltages drives through $R_s$. The power it takes is that current times its
own voltage <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8 (Maximum Power Transfer)" />:

<Eq id="src.cv_power" />

As a share of the available power
(<EqRef id="src.Pmax" label="maximum power from a linear source" />):

<Eq id="src.cv_extraction" />

It takes the whole of it only at half the open-circuit voltage. A battery's
voltage is set by its charge, not by the source, and a harvester's
amplitude varies: connected straight, the two rarely meet at that point.

### A converter in between

A converter between the source and the battery separates their voltages:
its input can sit where the source gives the most while its output stays at
the battery's voltage. Ottman et al. ran a step-down converter in DCM
between a piezoelectric element and a battery at the duty ratio that gives
the most power, and report that the converter increases the harvested power
"by approximately 325%" <Cite key="ottman2003" />.

### The loss-free resistor

In DCM with a fixed duty ratio, a buck-boost or flyback draws an average
input current proportional to its input voltage: its input is a resistance,
the <EqRef id="lfr.R_in" label="loss-free resistor" />, which passes the
power on instead of dissipating it <Cite key="singer1990" />
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15 (AC and DC Equivalent Circuit Modeling of the Discontinuous Conduction Mode)" />.
With a linear source, that resistance and the source resistance divide the
source voltage:

<Eq id="lfr.Vg" />

and the converter takes the same share of the available power at any source
amplitude <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8 (Maximum Power Transfer)" />:

<Eq id="lfr.eta" />

The share is the whole of it when the input resistance equals the source
resistance, and falls slowly on either side. A fixed duty ratio therefore
keeps a linear source at its maximum power point as its amplitude changes,
without measuring anything, as long as the converter stays in DCM
([the DCM flyback](../lfr-dcm-flyback/)).

## Bench exercise

Build a linear source from a bench supply and a series power resistor.
Connect an electronic load in constant-voltage mode, a battery's stand-in,
step its voltage from zero to the supply's, and record the current at each
step. Plot the power against the voltage and find where it peaks; compare
with half the supply's voltage. Then replace the load with a converter whose
duty ratio is fixed in DCM, and measure its input voltage and current at two
supply voltages: their ratio, the input resistance, stays the same.

## Gotchas

- **A battery connected straight to a source works at the battery's voltage,**
  not at the source's best (<EqRef id="src.cv_extraction" label="constant-voltage extraction" />).
- **The loss-free resistor holds only in DCM** (<EqRef id="lfr.R_in" label="loss-free resistor" />);
  a strong source can drive the converter into CCM
  ([the DCM flyback](../lfr-dcm-flyback/)).
- **Half the open-circuit voltage is the best point of a linear source only.**
  A source that is not linear has its own
  ([MPPT](../sece-sshi-mppt/#tracking-the-maximum-power-point)).
- **A fixed resistance keeps its share as the amplitude changes; a fixed voltage does not**
  (<EqRef id="lfr.eta" label="extraction by a loss-free resistor" />).
