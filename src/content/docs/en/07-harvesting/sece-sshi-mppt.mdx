## Intent

- Compare what a full-wave bridge, SECE and parallel SSHI take from the same vibrating piezoelectric element.
- Know which of them depend on the DC voltage behind them, and where each gives the most.
- Track the maximum power point of a source by a fraction of its open-circuit voltage.

## Theory

All three interfaces below take their power from the element of
[source models](../source-models/): a current source $I_p \sin \omega t$
beside its clamped capacitance $C_0$, vibrating at an amplitude that the
harvest does not change. Each half period, between two extrema of the
displacement, the source drives the same charge. The two interfaces with a
bridge differ in how much of it they spend swinging the element's voltage
before the bridge conducts; SECE lets all of it charge the element and then
takes the energy stored there.

### The standard interface

A full-wave bridge onto a DC voltage, a storage capacitor or a battery,
conducts only once the element's voltage has swung from one rail to the
other. That swing takes part of the charge each half period, and the rest
enters the DC side <Cite key="guyomar2005" />:

open-circuit amplitude, where the swing takes all of the charge, and it is

### SECE

Synchronous electric charge extraction leaves the element open between the
extrema of its voltage, then takes its whole charge at each extremum through
a converter into the storage <Cite key="lefeuvre2005" />. In between, the
whole charge of the half period raises the element's voltage, from zero to
twice its open-circuit amplitude, and the converter takes the energy then
stored in $C_0$ and delivers it at whatever voltage the storage has. The
element never connects to the storage, so the power does not depend on the
storage's voltage:

<Eq id="piezo.P_sece" />

That is four times the standard interface's best. Lefeuvre et al. report
that, over a standard interface adapted for the most power, the technique
"increases the harvested power by 400%" <Cite key="lefeuvre2005" />, that is, to four times.
The converter must take the element's voltage at extraction, twice its
open-circuit amplitude.

### Parallel SSHI

Synchronized switch harvesting on inductor, in its parallel form, keeps the
bridge and adds a switch and an inductor across the element. At each extremum
of the displacement the switch closes briefly, and the inductor inverts the
element's voltage <Cite key="guyomar2005" />. The inversion loses some of
it: with the element going from the DC voltage to a fraction $\gamma$ of it
of the opposite sign, the swing to the other rail costs only the rest:

<Eq id="piezo.P_sshi_max" />

The better the inversion, the more power, at a higher DC voltage. Guyomar et
al. report that compared with the standard technique the harvested power "may
be increased above 900%" <Cite key="guyomar2005" />; in this model that is an
inversion that keeps about four fifths of the voltage. Even an inversion
that keeps nothing, a switch that only empties the element, doubles the
standard interface's best.

### Tracking the maximum power point

For a linear source, the power into a constant voltage is largest at half
the open-circuit voltage (<EqRef id="src.cv_extraction" label="constant-voltage extraction" />),
and the standard interface above is also best at half the element's
open-circuit amplitude. A converter that holds its input at a fixed fraction
of the source's open-circuit voltage therefore stays at the maximum power
point as the source's amplitude changes: the fractional open-circuit voltage
method <Cite key="esram2007" />. To learn the open-circuit voltage, such a
converter stops drawing power now and then and samples it: one harvesting
charger periodically disables itself and samples a fraction of the
open-circuit voltage, typically 50 % for thermoelectric generators <Cite key="ti_bq25570" />. A source that is not linear, such as a solar cell,
has its best point at another fraction <Cite key="esram2007" />.

## Bench exercise

On a shaker at a steady amplitude, connect the element through a full-wave
bridge to a storage capacitor held at the DC voltage by a source-measure
unit, which sinks the bridge's current, microamperes, and measures it. Step
the DC voltage from zero to the open-circuit amplitude, recording the
current the unit sinks, and plot the power; compare the best DC voltage
with half the open-circuit amplitude. For parallel SSHI, add the
switch and inductor, trigger the switch at each voltage extremum, and read
the element's voltage just before and just after the inversion on the
oscilloscope: their ratio is the inversion coefficient to put in the formula.

## Gotchas

- **Above the open-circuit amplitude a bridge harvests nothing.** A storage
  charged beyond it stops charging (<EqRef id="piezo.P_std" label="power of the standard interface" />).
- **The equations take an ideal bridge.** In a real one two diodes conduct
  in series <Cite key="mohan2003" where="Ch. 5 (Line-Frequency Diode Rectifiers)" />,
  so the element must swing to the DC voltage plus two forward drops before
  the bridge conducts: for an element whose open-circuit amplitude is a few
  volts, a large part of the swing.
- **SECE's converter sees twice the open-circuit amplitude,** since the
  element's voltage climbs from zero across a whole half period
  (<EqRef id="piezo.P_sece" label="power of SECE" />).
- **SSHI's gain is the inversion's quality.** Losses in the switch and the
  inductor lower the coefficient, and the power with it
  (<EqRef id="piezo.P_sshi_max" label="largest power of parallel SSHI" />).
- **Sampling the open-circuit voltage costs harvest time:** the converter
  takes no power while it samples.
- **These results keep the vibration's amplitude fixed.** A harvest whose
  damping is not small against the structure's own losses lowers the
  amplitude, and the model then overstates every interface.
