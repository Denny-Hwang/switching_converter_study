## Intent

- Model a harvester that is linear over its working range as an open-circuit voltage behind a source resistance, and know the most power it can give.
- Model a vibrating piezoelectric element as a current source beside its clamped capacitance, and find the resistor that takes the most power from it.
- Know what a triboelectric generator's figure of merit compares.

## Theory

### A linear source

A source that is linear over the range it works in looks, to its load, like
an open-circuit voltage $V_\mathrm{oc}$ behind a source resistance $R_s$: its
Thevenin equivalent <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 4" />.
It delivers the most power into a load equal to its own resistance
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8 (Maximum Power Transfer)" />:

That is the power available from the source. A converter behind it takes a
share of it that depends on what its input looks like; the
[matching](../matching/) module works that share out.

### A vibrating piezoelectric element

A piezoelectric element on a structure that vibrates sinusoidally drives a
current proportional to the structure's velocity. With its clamped
capacitance $C_0$ across its terminals, it is a current source
$I_p \sin \omega t$ in parallel with $C_0$ <Cite key="guyomar2005" />, as
long as what the electrical side takes does not change the vibration's
amplitude. With nothing connected, the whole current charges and discharges
$C_0$, and the voltage swings each way by

<Eq id="piezo.VM" />

Its internal impedance is a capacitance, not a resistance. A resistor across
the element shares the current with $C_0$, and takes the most power when it
equals the capacitance's reactance at the vibration's frequency:

<Eq id="piezo.R_opt" />

<Eq id="piezo.P_R" />

The best resistor depends on the frequency: a resistor sized for one
vibration is mismatched at another. The energy the load takes comes out of
the vibration, so the model holds while that energy is small against the
structure's; where it is not, the harvest damps the vibration and the model
overstates the power.

A storage element needs a DC voltage, so the element usually works through
a rectifier. A full-wave bridge onto the best DC voltage gives less than the
best resistor; the nonlinear interfaces in [SECE, SSHI and MPPT](../sece-sshi-mppt/)
give more (<EqRef id="piezo.P_std_max" label="largest power of the standard interface" />).

### A triboelectric generator

Zi et al. proposed to compare triboelectric nanogenerators by the largest
energy a cycle of their motion can deliver, their cycles for maximized
energy output, per area and per displacement. Their performance figure of
merit is a structural figure of merit, set by the design, times the square
of the surface charge density, set by the materials <Cite key="zi2015" />.
It rates the generator by energy per cycle, not by the power a particular
load happens to take.

## Bench exercise

Mount the element on a shaker driven at a steady sinusoidal amplitude.
Measure its open-circuit voltage amplitude with a probe whose input
resistance is far above the best resistor, and note the probe's input
capacitance, which adds to $C_0$ ([probes](../../06-bench/probes/)). Then
load the element with a decade resistance box, record the voltage amplitude
at each value and compute the power each resistor takes. Find the resistor
that takes the most, and compare it with the reactance of the element's
capacitance at the vibration's frequency, measured with an LCR meter. Repeat
at a second frequency and see the best resistor move.

## Gotchas

- **A probe loads a high-impedance source.** Its input resistance and
  capacitance sit in parallel with the source; compare them with the best
  resistor (<EqRef id="piezo.R_opt" label="best resistor" />) and with
  $C_0$ before trusting a reading.
- **The best resistor holds at one frequency.** It is the reactance of $C_0$
  at the vibration's frequency, and moves with it.
- **A bridge onto a DC voltage takes less than the best resistor**
  (<EqRef id="piezo.P_std_max" label="largest power of the standard interface" />).
- **The model does not damp the vibration.** A load that takes a large part
  of the structure's energy lowers the vibration's amplitude, and the model
  then overstates the power.
