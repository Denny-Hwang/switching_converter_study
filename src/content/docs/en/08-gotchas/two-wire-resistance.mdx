## Symptom

The dc resistance of a winding with few turns, or of any low resistance,
reads well above its calculated value
(<EqRef id="wind.dcr" label="dc resistance of a winding" />), and the
reading changes when the probes are pressed harder or moved.

## Why

A two-wire ohmmeter passes its test current through its leads and reads
the voltage across the same leads, so the reading is the part together
with the leads and their contacts. The four-wire (Kelvin) method passes the
current through one pair of leads and senses the voltage with another, so
the leads' resistance stays out of the reading <Cite key="keithley_llmh7" />.
A winding of a few turns can have a resistance well below that of the leads.

## How to confirm

Touch the two probes together and read: that is the leads and their
contacts alone. If it is not small against the part's reading, the reading
is mostly leads. Then measure the part with four wires: the difference
between the two readings is the leads and their contacts.

## Fix

Measure with four wires: the current leads on the part's terminals, and the
sense leads on the same terminals, so that only the part lies between the
sense points <Cite key="keithley_llmh7" />. An impedance meter's
four-terminal connections keep the leads' impedance out in the same way
<Cite key="keysight_5950_3000" />. Note the winding's temperature with the
reading: copper's resistance rises with it
(<EqRef id="wind.rho_T" label="resistivity at temperature" />).
