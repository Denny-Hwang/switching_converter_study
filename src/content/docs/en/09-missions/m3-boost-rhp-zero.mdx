## Goal

- Explain in your own words why a step of the boost's duty ratio first moves its output the wrong way.
- Compute the right-half-plane (RHP) zero and the double pole of the boost example.
- Find how the zero moves with the load and the duty ratio, and which operating point limits a feedback loop's crossover frequency.

### 5. The operating point that limits the loop

Over a range of loads and input voltages, the zero is lowest at the heaviest
load and the largest duty ratio, that is, at the lowest input voltage. A
loop's crossover frequency must stay well below that lowest zero
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />. Say which corner of your range
that is for the example, and why designing at the nominal point would
overestimate the crossover you may use.

## Gotchas

- $\omega_z$ is in rad/s. Taking it for hertz puts the crossover limit more than six times too high.
- The zero moves with the operating point. The nominal point is not the worst case: the heaviest load at the lowest input voltage is.
- A compensator cannot cancel an RHP zero. That would take a pole in the right half-plane, which is unstable <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 8" />.
