When a flyback's switch turns off, the magnetizing current passes to the
secondary. The current in the leakage inductance cannot: it drives the drain
above the input voltage plus the reflected output voltage until a clamp
across the primary conducts <Cite key="ti_ssztcv6" />. The clamp check takes
the worst-case operating point and the clamp's data, all in SI units. It
shows:

- the reflected voltage, the leakage energy and the time the leakage
  current takes to fall to zero;
- the clamp voltage at the actual peak current, and the switch voltage
  against its rating;
- the clamp dissipation;
- with a TVS, the output voltage the flyback runs up to without a load.

Each result names its equation. The chart shows how the dissipation and the
switch voltage trade against the clamp voltage.

## What it computes

Enter the largest input voltage and the largest peak primary current at
turn-off. If they occur at different operating points, checking them
together is conservative. The turns ratio is 1:$n$, where $n$ is the
secondary turns divided by the primary turns, and $L_\mathrm{lk}$ is the
leakage inductance referred to the primary.

### Reflected voltage

While the output diode conducts, the primary winding carries the output
voltage plus the diode drop, divided by $n$
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 6" />:

<Eq id="flyback.V_OR" />

A clamp must hold a higher voltage than this. Otherwise it conducts whenever
the output diode does, and takes the energy meant for the output.

### Leakage energy

At turn-off the leakage inductance carries the peak primary current
(<EqRef id="flyback.leak.E" label="leakage energy" />). The clamp takes that
energy in every period (<EqRef id="flyback.leak.P" label="leakage power" />).

### Clamp voltage

A **TVS** starts to conduct at its breakdown voltage $V_\mathrm{BR}$, and its
voltage rises with its current. Its datasheet gives the clamping voltage
$V_\mathrm{CL}$ at a peak pulse current $I_\mathrm{PP}$. A dynamic
resistance in series with the breakdown voltage models the rise between them
<Cite key="st_an316" />:

<Eq id="tvs.R_D" />

The tool takes the TVS's voltage at the peak primary current as the highest
clamp voltage:

<Eq id="tvs.V_clamp" />

While the leakage current falls to zero, the TVS voltage falls back towards
$V_\mathrm{BR}$, so the tool shows the clamp voltage as a range.

An **RCD clamp** is a diode into a capacitor with a resistor across it. The
capacitor holds the clamp voltage, and the resistor dissipates the clamp's
power. The clamp voltage settles where the two balance (the root above
$V_\mathrm{OR}$; see the derivation linked under the equation):

<Eq id="clamp.rcd.V" />

By this equation a larger resistor gives a higher clamp voltage, and by the
dissipation equation below a lower dissipation: the clamp trades its voltage
against its losses <Cite key="ti_ssztcv6" />.

### Switch voltage

While the clamp conducts, the switch blocks the input voltage plus the clamp
voltage:

<Eq id="clamp.Vds" />

The margin is the switch's rating minus this voltage, at the highest clamp
voltage.

### Reset time and clamp dissipation

While the clamp conducts, the magnetizing inductance holds the primary at
$V_\mathrm{OR}$. The leakage inductance therefore sees
$V_\mathrm{clamp} - V_\mathrm{OR}$, and its current falls linearly to zero
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />:

<Eq id="clamp.t_reset" />

The reset must fit in the off-time. The closer the clamp voltage is to
$V_\mathrm{OR}$, the longer it takes; the tool warns when it would take a
whole switching period or more. The clamp would then conduct energy meant
for the output, and the equations here no longer hold.

Meanwhile the reflected voltage keeps driving current into the clamp. So the
clamp absorbs more than the leakage energy, and the closer the clamp voltage
is to $V_\mathrm{OR}$, the more it absorbs <Cite key="ti_ssztcv6" />:

<Eq id="clamp.P" />

The dissipation falls as the clamp voltage rises. For a TVS, this equation at
the highest clamp voltage is a lower bound, and at $V_\mathrm{BR}$ an upper
bound. The table shows both, and their ratio to the leakage power.

### Output voltage without a load

With the load removed and no regulation, the output keeps charging. It
stops only when its reflected voltage reaches the clamp voltage, and from
then on the clamp takes all of the input power. A TVS holds its voltage
while it does, so the output stops at:

<Eq id="flyback.V_ceiling" />

The output capacitor and everything on the output must withstand this
voltage, and the TVS must be rated for the whole input power, far more than
its dissipation in normal operation.

An RCD clamp gives no such bound. Its voltage is where its resistor
dissipates the clamp's power (the power balance behind the RCD equation
above), so at no load, with the whole input power to dissipate, the clamp
voltage rises far above its loaded value. The output and the switch voltage
rise with it. The tool shows no ceiling for an RCD clamp and warns instead:
limit the input power at no load, or add a fixed-voltage clamp.

## Choosing the clamp voltage

The chart plots the dissipation (logarithmic axis) and the switch voltage
against the clamp voltage, from just above $V_\mathrm{OR}$. A low clamp
voltage costs dissipation, and a high one costs switch voltage. The switch's
rating sets the highest clamp voltage the switch allows. The shaded band is
the range of the clamp entered.

## Limitations

- **Ideal clamp.** The clamp holds a constant voltage while it conducts (for
  the RCD clamp, the capacitor's ripple is small), and the leakage current
  falls linearly. The off-time is not an input: the tool compares the reset
  time with the whole switching period only. The clamp diode's reverse recovery, and the ringing of the
  leakage inductance with the switch-node capacitance after the clamp stops,
  are not modelled.
- **TVS data.** The dynamic resistance derived from the datasheet's
  $V_\mathrm{CL}$ at $I_\mathrm{PP}$ applies to that datasheet pulse: it
  depends on the pulse duration, and the breakdown voltage rises with
  temperature <Cite key="st_an316" />. Take the datasheet
  values over the design's conditions, not the typical ones, and check the
  TVS's power rating for its repetitive dissipation.
- **Dissipative clamps only.** The energy is turned into heat. Clamps that
  return it to the input or the output are not covered.
