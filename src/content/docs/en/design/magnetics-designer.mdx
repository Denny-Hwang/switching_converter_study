Pick an inductor or a flyback transformer, a core and the windings. The two
table cores carry the values TDK publishes for them in its N87 ferrite
<Cite key="tdk_e25" />, <Cite key="tdk_etd29" />. For any other core, choose
"Other core" and enter the values from its data sheet. The designer gives:

- the fewest turns that keep the peak flux density within a limit, and the
  air gap that then gives the inductance;
- for a table core, the gapped core sets its data sheet offers, with the
  turns each one needs;
- the peak flux density, and the ac flux amplitude that the core loss
  depends on;
- the window utilization, and each winding's dc resistance and ac resistance
  factor at the switching frequency;
- for a flyback, the leakage inductance of the winding arrangement.

Each result names its equation. The chart shows each winding's ac resistance
factor against frequency.

## What it computes

Enter the inductance and the peak and rms current of the winding. For a
flyback these are the magnetizing inductance referred to the primary and the
primary's currents, and the secondary's rms current is entered separately.
The [converter designer](../converter-designer/) gives the inductance and the
peak current. The ripple $\Delta i$ is half the peak-to-peak ripple, as
everywhere on this site. $B_\mathrm{max}$ is the largest flux density the
design allows: below the material's saturation flux density at the core's
highest temperature, with a margin <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 11" />.

### Turns

The flux density follows the current
(<EqRef id="mag.B_pk" label="peak flux density" />). Holding its peak at
$B_\mathrm{max}$ gives the fewest turns
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 11" />:

<Eq id="mag.N_Bmax" />

The tool evaluates it at the core's smallest cross-section $A_\mathrm{min}$,
where the flux density is highest, and rounds up to whole turns. More turns
lower the flux density further, but lengthen the winding and raise its
resistance. Enter $N$ to use more.

### The gap

With the turns fixed, the inductance sets the gap. The magnetic circuit of a
gapped core (<EqRef id="mag.AL_gap" label="inductance factor of a gapped core" />),
solved for the gap length, gives:

<Eq id="mag.gap_length" />

Here $\mu_i$ is the effective permeability of the ungapped set. The tool
takes it from the data sheet's $A_L$ of the ungapped set, through the same
equation with no gap. A negative result means that even the ungapped core
gives less than the inductance with these turns: add turns.

This estimate ignores fringing. Around the gap the flux spreads beyond the
core's area, so a real gap passes more flux than the estimate assumes, and
it must be longer for the same inductance. A data sheet therefore gives the
$A_L$ of its gapped core sets, each a gapped core with an ungapped one
<Cite key="tdk_e25" />. For a table core the tool lists these sets. For each
it shows the turns that give the inductance, the inductance of those whole
turns and the peak flux density at $A_\mathrm{min}$. The button in a row
uses its turns. Compare a set's gap with the gap the tool estimates for the
same turns: at the larger gaps, the real gap is clearly longer.

### Flux density and its swing

The peak flux density at $A_\mathrm{min}$ is what must stay below
saturation. The core loss depends on the swing instead. A ripple of
$\pm\Delta i$ moves the flux density about its dc value by:

<Eq id="mag.B_ac" />

This is the amplitude that core-loss data take
(<EqRef id="loss.steinmetz" label="core loss per volume" />): read the loss
per volume off the material's loss curves at $B_\mathrm{ac}$ and $f_s$, or
use the [loss budget](../loss-budget/). In a CCM inductor the ripple is a
small part of the current, and so is $B_\mathrm{ac}$ of $B_\mathrm{pk}$.
In a DCM flyback the current falls to zero every period, and the swing is
the whole peak.

### AC resistance (Dowell)

At high frequency the current crowds into a surface layer about a skin depth
thick <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />:

<Eq id="wind.skin_depth" />

In a winding of several layers, each layer also carries the field of the
layers below it, which induces eddy currents in it: the proximity effect.
Dowell's analysis treats each layer as a foil <Cite key="dowell1966" />. For
round wire, each strand becomes a square of the same area, and the layer
becomes a foil of that thickness whose gaps lower its conductivity
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 10" />:

<Eq id="wind.porosity" />

<Eq id="wind.phi_round" />

<Eq id="wind.dowell" />

$F_R$ is the ratio of the ac resistance to $R_\mathrm{dc}$ at one frequency.
Each harmonic of the current sees the factor at its own frequency, so the
chart plots $F_R$ against frequency, with $f_s$ marked. With $\varphi$ above
1, each added layer raises $F_R$ steeply, as $M_\ell^2$. The remedies are
fewer layers of thinner strands in parallel, or a foil.

In an inductor with a small ripple, the ac part of the current is small and
$F_R$ matters little: the table's dc loss is then most of the copper loss. A
flyback's windings carry pulses, so much of each winding's rms current is
ac. Its harmonics see $F_R$ at their frequencies, and the copper loss can be
several times the dc loss.

## Limitations

- **Round wire only.** Foil and litz windings are not covered. Dowell's
  factor assumes a one-dimensional field across the layers
  <Cite key="dowell1966" />. Near a gap the field is two-dimensional, and the
  factor does not describe the loss there.
- **Fringing.** The gap estimate ignores it (see above). For a table core,
  use the data sheet's gapped sets.
- **Flyback windings.** The two windings conduct in turn, so their fields do
  not cancel as a transformer's do. Each winding's $F_R$ is taken with its own
  layers, from zero field on one side. The eddy-current loss that a
  winding's field induces in the idle winding is not modelled.
- **No core loss and no thermal model.** The tool gives $B_\mathrm{ac}$ for
  the material's loss data, and takes the winding temperature as entered.
- **Core data.** The ungapped set's $A_L$ has a wide tolerance, +30/−20 %
  <Cite key="tdk_e25" />, and the gap estimate follows it.
