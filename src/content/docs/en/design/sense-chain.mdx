A converter measures its current with a shunt resistor, whose small voltage
an amplifier raises for an ADC. Each stage sets a limit. The shunt takes
voltage and power from the circuit it measures. The amplifier runs out of
output range at the top, and adds its offset at the bottom. The pads and
solder in the sense path add resistance of their own, and the ADC folds
anything above half its sampling rate back into the reading. The sense
chain takes the whole chain, in SI units, from zero to the largest current:

- the gain, the full-scale current and which limit sets it, and the output
  floor;
- the sense voltage (the burden) and the shunt's dissipation at the largest
  current;
- the offset-equivalent current, the pad error, and the error of the
  reading at the smallest current;
- the R-C filter's corner against the Nyquist frequency.

It flags each violation, and each result names its equation. Two charts show
the output and the relative error against the current.

## What it computes

The tool measures currents from zero up to $I_\mathrm{max}$, and
$\varepsilon_\mathrm{max}$ is the error you accept at the smallest current
that matters, $I_\mathrm{min}$.

### Sense voltage and burden

By Ohm's law the shunt turns the current into the sense voltage
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 2" />:

<Eq id="sense.burden" />

This voltage is the amplifier's signal, and also the burden: the voltage the
shunt takes from the circuit it measures. A larger shunt gives a larger
signal, and costs more burden and more dissipation, the shunt's
<EqRef id="loss.cond" label="conduction loss" /> at the largest current. The
tool compares them with the circuit's allowance and the shunt's power rating
when you enter them.

### The amplifier's output

A **current-output amplifier** forces the sense voltage across its input
resistor $R_\mathrm{IN}$. The resulting current flows out through
$R_\mathrm{OUT}$, which turns it back into a voltage to ground
<Cite key="adi_ltc6101" />:

<Eq id="sense.current_out_monitor" />

The two resistors set the gain.

A **voltage-output amplifier** has a fixed gain $G$, and adds the voltage on
its REF pin <Cite key="ti_ina181" where="Equation 1" />:

<Eq id="sense.voltage_out_monitor" />

The REF voltage sets the output at zero current. A current of either sign
then swings the output to either side of it, and a REF voltage of zero suits
a current that flows one way only.

### Full scale and the output floor

Two limits cap the output. The amplifier delivers only up to its largest
output voltage $V_\mathrm{OUT,max}$, which depends on its supply; the ADC
reads only up to its full scale $V_\mathrm{FS}$. A voltage-output
amplifier's data sheet gives its output swing as a small voltage short of
the supply rail and above ground <Cite key="ti_ina181" />. The LTC6101 data
sheet gives its largest output as fixed values <Cite key="adi_ltc6101" />.
Enter the values that apply at your supply.

The tool solves the output equation for the current at which the output
reaches the lower of the two limits: that is the full-scale current. If it
lies below $I_\mathrm{max}$, the reading clips before the largest current.
If it lies far above, the ADC's range is partly wasted; the table gives the
share of the full scale that the largest current uses.

At the bottom, a voltage-output amplifier with its REF pin at zero cannot
drive its output all the way to ground. Its lowest output
$V_\mathrm{OUT,min}$, the swing to ground, sets a floor: every current whose
output would fall below it reads the same. The tool gives the current below
which that happens, and flags a floor above $I_\mathrm{min}$. A REF voltage
above the floor removes it.

### Offset

The amplifier's input offset voltage adds directly to the sense voltage
<Cite key="adi_ltc6101" where="Applications Information" />. So the output
reads it as a current, the same at every current:

<Eq id="sense.offset_current" />

A larger shunt lowers this current, because the same current then gives a
larger sense voltage <Cite key="ti_ina181" />. The price is more burden and
more dissipation.

### Pad and solder resistance

With a shunt of a few milliohms or less, the resistance of the solder
becomes a substantial part of the sense element's resistance, and adds to
the measurement error <Cite key="osullivan2012" />. If the sense
connections sit beyond the pads, the amplifier sees the load current through
the shunt and the pads together, $R_\mathrm{SENSE} + R_\mathrm{pad}$. So the reading is high by
the same fraction at every current:

<Eq id="sense.pad_error" />

A Kelvin connection takes the pads out of the sense path: a four-terminal
shunt, or a two-terminal one on a four-pad (split-pad) layout
<Cite key="osullivan2012" />. The LTC6101 data sheet recommends Kelvin
connections to the sense resistor in all but the lowest-power applications
<Cite key="adi_ltc6101" where="Applications Information" />. With a Kelvin
connection, enter $R_\mathrm{pad}$ as zero.

### Error at the smallest current

With both the offset and the pad resistance, the two errors add, with no
cross term:

<Eq id="sense.rel_error" />

The offset's part falls in inverse proportion to the current, so it matters
most at $I_\mathrm{min}$. The pad's part is the same at every current. The
tool flags each part that alone exceeds $\varepsilon_\mathrm{max}$ at
$I_\mathrm{min}$, and flags the sum when only the two together do. The
error chart plots both against the current, with the target.

### Filter and sampling

An ADC that samples at the rate $f_\mathrm{samp}$ represents the band from
dc to the Nyquist frequency:

<Eq id="adc.nyquist" />

Any component above it aliases back into that band, so an anti-aliasing
filter removes it before the ADC <Cite key="kester_mt002" />. In a
converter, the switching ripple is such a component. The tool takes a
first-order R-C filter, with its
<EqRef id="rc.fc" label="corner frequency" /> and
<EqRef id="rc.gain" label="gain magnitude" /> from the phasors page.
It flags a corner at or above the Nyquist frequency, and gives the filter's
gain at the Nyquist frequency. That check is the tool's own rule, built on
the Nyquist criterion. A single pole attenuates only in inverse proportion
to the frequency, so a corner below the Nyquist frequency is the least to
ask for, and not always enough: the gain at the Nyquist frequency tells how
much still passes there.

A current-output amplifier's output is a current source, and $R_\mathrm{OUT}$
turns it into a voltage. Seen from the filter, that is a source with
$R_\mathrm{OUT}$ behind it, its Thevenin equivalent
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Ch. 4" />. So the filter's capacitor sees
$R_\mathrm{OUT}$ in series with $R_f$ (the derivation is linked under the
equation):

<Eq id="sense.filter_R" />

A capacitor straight across $R_\mathrm{OUT}$, with no $R_f$, is a filter of
this kind too. A voltage-output amplifier drives the filter from a low
impedance, so there the capacitor sees $R_f$ alone.

## Limitations

- **Offset and pads only.** The error model has the amplifier's input offset
  and the pad resistance. It leaves out the amplifier's gain error, the
  tolerance of the gain resistors, the input bias currents, the common-mode
  rejection and noise. It also leaves out the shunt's own tolerance and its
  change with temperature, and the ADC's own errors. A real error budget
  adds them.
- **Currents from zero up.** The chain is checked for a current that flows
  one way. The shunt's dissipation is that of a dc current at
  $I_\mathrm{max}$. A varying current dissipates by its rms value, while
  the output limits apply to its peak.
- **Output limits as entered.** The output range depends on the supply, the
  load and the temperature. Take the data sheet's limits at your conditions.
- **An unloaded first-order filter.** The ADC's input draws charge when it
  samples. Its data sheet gives the source impedance it allows.
- **One pad resistance.** For the lowest-value shunts, where the sense point
  sits on the pad and how evenly the current spreads through the resistor
  matter too <Cite key="osullivan2012" />.
