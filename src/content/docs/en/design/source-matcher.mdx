A linear source is an open-circuit voltage $V_\mathrm{oc}$ behind a source
resistance $R_s$, and it delivers the most power into a matched load
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8" />. A flyback in DCM with a fixed
duty ratio draws power from its input like a resistor, so it can be matched
to such a source <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15" />. The source matcher takes the
source, the flyback and its fixed output, all in SI units. It shows:

- the operating point: the bus voltage, the power drawn, and its share of
  the available power;
- that share against the source amplitude, where CCM takes over;
- the switch voltage that a larger power needs;
- optionally, the flyback simulated cycle by cycle over a varying source
  amplitude.

Each result names its equation. The link under the table opens the
operating point in the [simulator](../../simulate/simulator/).

### The flyback as a resistor

In DCM with a fixed duty ratio, the flyback's average input current is
proportional to its input voltage: its input is a resistance, the
<EqRef id="lfr.R_in" label="loss-free resistor" />, which passes the power
on instead of dissipating it <Cite key="singer1990" />
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 15" />. With
a linear source, this resistance and the source resistance divide the source
voltage:

<Eq id="lfr.Vg" />

The flyback takes the same share of the available power at any source
amplitude, the whole of it when its resistance matches the source's
<Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8" />:

<Eq id="lfr.eta" />

### Where CCM takes over

The flyback stays in DCM while its input is below the
<EqRef id="flyback.V_crit" label="critical input voltage" />. A source strong
enough to raise the divider voltage above it drives the flyback into CCM,
and CCM holds the bus at the critical voltage. The flyback then acts as a
constant-voltage sink, whose share of the available power depends on the
source amplitude <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8" />:

<Eq id="src.cv_extraction" />

The tool finds the source amplitude at which this starts by solving the
divider equation for $V_\mathrm{oc}$. The extraction chart shows both regimes.
While the flyback is a resistor, the share is flat. Once CCM holds the bus,
the share is the constant-voltage sink's, which is 1 where the source
amplitude is twice the critical voltage and falls beyond. If $R_\mathrm{in}$
is larger than $R_s$, the bus reaches the critical voltage below that
amplitude, and the share first rises to 1.

### More power needs a higher switch voltage, up to the critical voltage

Through the same input resistance, a larger power needs a higher bus
voltage:

<Eq id="lfr.Vg_power" />

The switch blocks the bus voltage plus the reflected output voltage
(<EqRef id="flyback.Vds_off" label="switch voltage" />). That holds up to the
critical voltage: there CCM holds the bus, and the switch voltage stays at
<EqRef id="flyback.Vds_clamped" label="its CCM value" /> whatever power the
source then pushes in. The largest power the flyback draws as a resistor is
therefore <EqRef id="dcm.P_in" label="the power a DCM converter draws" /> at
the critical voltage. If the switch's rating lies below the CCM value, the
rating caps it earlier, where the switch voltage reaches the rating; the
tool says which limit applies. The switch chart plots the switch voltage
against the power: the resistor's rising branch, then the CCM plateau.

### A varying source amplitude

With an envelope, the source amplitude follows a rectified sine or points
you enter, as a fraction of $V_\mathrm{oc}$. The tool then simulates the flyback
cycle by cycle with the [simulator](../../simulate/simulator/)'s
source-driven model, each switching cycle at the source amplitude of its
start, so an envelope period must span at least twenty switching cycles.

The run starts from an empty bus, seven slowest time constants of the bus
before an envelope period. While the flyback is a resistor, that time
constant is at most $R_s C_\mathrm{bus}$; once CCM holds the bus, the averaged
bus and magnetizing inductance form a second-order system
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 7" />, whose slower mode can take much
longer, with a stiff source above all. The tool then repeats the
envelope period until it repeats itself, and shows the last one. Its bus
voltage and power are averaged over each switching cycle.

The power drawn is the power the source delivers into the bus at the bus
voltage <Cite key="alexander2017" where="Sec. 4.8" />:

<Eq id="src.cv_power" />

It is negative while the bus feeds the source back, when the source
amplitude falls below the bus voltage. Its sum over the period, divided by
the available energy, gives the share over the envelope. The number of
switching cycles in a run is limited, to keep it within a few seconds; the
tool says so when an envelope would need more.

## Worked example

<Worked example="source-lfr" />

Here the flyback's resistance equals the source resistance, so it takes all
of the available power, <Val example="source-lfr" name="P_max" />, at a bus
voltage of <Val example="source-lfr" name="V_g" />. That is well below the
critical input voltage, <Val example="source-lfr" name="V_gcrit" />, so the
flyback stays in DCM, and its switch blocks
<Val example="source-lfr" name="V_DS" />. Raise $V_\mathrm{oc}$ in the tool until
the bus reaches the critical voltage: the flyback becomes a constant-voltage
sink, and its share of the available power falls. The
[simulator's worked example](../../simulate/simulator/#worked-example-a-flyback-that-pins-its-source) is this
flyback at a higher source amplitude, in that regime.

The "Rectified-sine envelope" example runs the same flyback over an envelope
whose peaks reach the constant-voltage regime. The bus follows the source
while the flyback is a resistor, and stays at the critical voltage near the
peaks.

## Limitations

- **Linear source.** The source is a constant voltage behind a constant
  resistance. A source whose resistance changes with its operating point is
  outside this model.
- **Ideal flyback.** Apart from the output diode's drop, the flyback is
  loss-free: the envelope simulation has no on-resistance, winding resistance
  or node capacitance. The output is a fixed voltage (a battery, for example).
  The switch voltage leaves out the spike from the leakage inductance at
  turn-off; the [clamp check](../clamp-check/) sizes the clamp for it.
- **Averaged bus.** The operating point and the charts use averages: they
  assume the bus capacitor holds the bus nearly constant over a switching
  period. With a small capacitor the bus ripples, and the tool says so; the
  simulator link shows the difference.
- **Slow envelope.** Each switching cycle sees a constant source amplitude,
  so the envelope must change little within one switching period.
