Pick a topology or a preset, choose the load, and set the parameters in SI
units: type a value, or drag its slider, which spans one decade either side
of the value (0 to 1 for the duty ratio). The simulator computes one period
of the periodic steady state and shows:

- the waveforms: inductor current and voltage, diode current, switch voltage
  and output voltage, and the battery's current when there is one;
- the conduction mode it detects, with $K$ and $K_\mathrm{crit}$;
- the simulated values next to the catalogue's equations at the same
  operating point;
- the losses and the efficiency computed from the waveforms, and where the
  load's power goes.

When the circuit has no periodic steady state, it says why and shows the
start-up from rest instead ([below](#when-there-is-no-steady-state)).

Everything runs in your browser on the `pe-core` engine that the test suite
validates. The URL holds the whole state, and the "Try it" links on the
learning pages open the simulator with a preset.

## What is modelled

Each converter is a set of linear circuits, one for each interval of the
switching period: switch on, switch off with the diode conducting, and, in
DCM, the idle interval <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />,
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 5" />.

- **Switches and diodes.** The transistor is an ideal switch with an optional
  on-resistance and an ideal body diode across it. The diode is a constant
  forward drop that blocks ideally, with no reverse recovery. The inductor
  can have a winding resistance. A current that would reverse through a
  diode stops instead: the forward converter's rectifier blocks while its
  output is above $n V_g$ less the diode drop. A negative current through
  the switch flows through its body diode, which conducts at zero volts:
  after turn-off until the current reaches zero, and while the switch is on
  too, when the diode takes the current from the on-resistance.
- **Transformers.** They are ideal (1:n with $n$ the secondary turns over
  the primary turns) except for the magnetizing inductance. There is no
  leakage inductance.
- **Transitions.** Without a capacitance across the switch they are
  instantaneous. With one, the inductor current charges it after turn-off
  until the diode takes over. While the diode conducts, the capacitance
  follows the switch voltage, which moves with the output and input ripple,
  so it takes its share of their capacitors' current. Once the diode current
  reaches zero, the inductance rings with the capacitance
  (<EqRef id="dcm.ring.f" label="ringing frequency" />). The switch's body
  diode clamps the ringing at zero switch voltage, and the energy in the
  capacitance is lost at every turn-on
  (<EqRef id="loss.sw.cap" label="capacitive turn-on loss" />). The input
  energy therefore equals the output energy plus the losses, also with the
  capacitance.
- **Source-driven mode.** The input can be a Thevenin source charging a bus
  capacitor instead of a fixed voltage. With a fixed output and a fixed duty
  ratio, this reproduces the constant-voltage sink of the flyback
  (<EqRef id="flyback.V_crit" label="the pinned input voltage" />).

## Loads

The output capacitor $C$ has across it one of:

- a resistor $R$;
- a battery: its open-circuit voltage $V_b$ behind its internal resistance
  $R_b$, the Rint model <Cite key="he2011" where="Sec. 2.1" />;
- a battery and a resistor;
- nothing, so that the capacitor charges from its start voltage $V_0$.

Or the output is an ideal fixed voltage $V$ (a stiff battery or a regulated
bus), with no capacitor. The battery's current is

<Eq id="bat.rint" />

It is positive while the battery charges. In a real battery the resistance
and the open-circuit voltage depend on the state of charge, the state of
health and the temperature <Cite key="he2011" where="Sec. 2.1" />; the
simulator holds both constant: a battery at one state of charge. Over the
periods it computes (a handful to find a steady state, a few hundred at
most for a start-up) the charge a battery takes is a tiny part of any real
battery's capacity. The load table below the charts splits the battery's
power into what its open-circuit voltage takes ($V_b$ times the battery's
average current) and what its internal resistance dissipates ($R_b$ times
the mean square of that current).

## When there is no steady state

A periodic steady state needs every state to return to its start at the end
of the period. For an inductor this is the volt-second balance
(<EqRef id="vsb.v_off" label="volt-second balance" />), for a capacitor the
charge balance (<EqRef id="csb.i_off" label="charge balance" />)
<Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 2" />. Some circuits cannot meet them,
and the simulator then says which state keeps changing, by how much per
cycle, and shows the start-up from rest (no current, the capacitor at its
start voltage or the battery's) in place of the steady-state period:

- **An inductor current that runs away.** A fixed output fed in CCM from a
  fixed input balances the inductor's volt-seconds at one duty ratio only.
  Above it, the average voltage the inductance sees over a cycle is
  positive, and the current grows by the same amount every cycle
  (<EqRef id="vsb.drift" label="change over one period" />). Below it, the
  current falls to zero within the cycle instead, and the converter settles
  in DCM. The message gives the change per cycle, that average voltage, and
  the duty ratio that would balance with ideal parts: the catalogue's CCM
  conversion ratio solved for $D$, with the fixed output over the input as
  the ratio (negative for the buck-boost, whose output is inverted). A
  flyback into a fixed output, for example, gains in each on-interval the
  rise that its magnetizing current has from zero in DCM
  (<EqRef id="flyback.Ipk.dcm" label="the on-interval's rise" />), and loses
  in the off-interval only what the output voltage, reflected to the
  primary, takes away in the time left. The forward converter's magnetizing
  current walks up in the same way above its reset limit
  (<EqRef id="forward.reset.Dmax" label="maximum duty ratio" />), which the
  message then names. At exactly the balancing duty ratio, with ideal parts, the
  volt-seconds balance whatever the current: every current that starts
  and ends a period at the same value is a steady state, and the simulator
  reports the one it reaches from its starting point. A duty ratio so close
  to it that the current's change per cycle is less than a millionth of how
  far it moves within the period passes the convergence test below, and
  counts as balanced.
- **A capacitor that charges without bound.** With nothing across it, the
  output capacitor keeps what every cycle delivers. Behind a boost,
  buck-boost or flyback without a capacitance across the switch, every cycle
  stores energy in the inductance and hands it on whatever the output's
  voltage, so the capacitor charges without bound. The message gives the
  voltage reached and the gain per cycle.
- **Anything else** that has not settled within the cycle limit, such as
  an undamped L-C ringing.

A capacitor alone does settle behind the other converters:

- **Behind a buck** it settles at the input's voltage, from below or from
  above, because the switch conducts both ways. The search starts there.
- **Behind a forward converter**, and behind a boost, buck-boost or flyback
  **with a capacitance across the switch**, it stops once no more charge
  reaches it: for the forward converter at about $n V_g$ less the diode drop
  (a little below it with $R_\mathrm{on}$) or above, since its rectifier
  lets no current back; for the others at the voltage to which the
  inductance can no longer lift the node capacitance. Every voltage above
  that one is a steady state too, and which one the capacitor keeps depends
  on its start-up: an L-C charge can overshoot and keep its peak, the first
  periods from rest can carry more energy than the periodic ringing, and an
  input bus that sags and recovers moves the voltage on the way. So the
  simulator follows the start-up from $V_0$ and searches from where the
  capacitor stops charging: no charge for 50 periods in a row, or for 3 with
  every other state repeating itself. A start-up that charges again after
  such a pause ends above the voltage reported. Where the charge per period
  instead shrinks steadily towards a limit, with every other state repeating
  itself, it searches for that limit, the lowest voltage that no charge
  reaches; the start-up, charging in ever smaller steps, stops just above it
  (by about a hundred-millionth of the voltage in the cases tried). It
  follows at most 4000 periods and two million sub-steps: with a node
  capacitance, whose ringing needs more sub-steps, that is 100 to 1000
  periods, and random trials often reach it. A start-up still charging then
  is searched from where it got to; if that search fails, or finds a voltage
  below the one the start-up reached, the steady state is found from above
  instead, with the capacitor high enough that no charge reaches it, and the
  voltage brought down to the lowest one that no charge reaches. Such a
  start-up is reported below the voltage it would stop at, by up to about
  0.07 % in random trials, and a steady state found from above can lie a
  few millionths above it. The periods followed count in the number of cycles
  the result reports.

Without a steady state there is no conduction mode, no comparison with the
equations and no table of the load or the losses: each of them describes a
periodic steady state.

## Operating modes

Below the charts the steady-state period is split into its modes, the way
converter papers describe a circuit mode by mode: each mode is a stretch of
the period in which one linear circuit holds (the switch on, the diode
conducting, the idle interval of DCM <Cite key="erickson2020" where="Ch. 5" />,
the node capacitance's rise and ringing, the forward converter's reset).
Pick a mode with its button, or click on the charts; the charts shade it.
For the selected mode the view shows:

- **the circuit**, with the branches that carry current in the mode in
  colour and the others grey, and an arrow on each coloured branch in the
  direction its current flows (two heads when the current reverses within
  the mode). The switch is drawn closed while its gate is on; its body
  diode, across it, is coloured when it carries the current. Next to each
  element are its name and its state;
- **what happens**, in a few sentences for each topology and mode;
- **a table** of every element's state, its current at the start and the
  end of the mode with its average over it, and its voltage where the
  simulator has it.

The states are read from the simulated waveforms of the mode, never
assumed: an inductor stores energy while its current's magnitude grows and
releases it while it shrinks; a capacitor or a battery charges while its
current flows into its positive terminal; a source delivers while its
current leaves its positive terminal; a current that changes sign within
the mode is reported with the instant it reverses. A current smaller than a
millionth of the period's largest current counts as none.

The drawing and the table use the same currents, and those come from the
model's outputs for each element: Kirchhoff's current law holds at every
node of the drawn circuit at every instant of the period, for every
topology, load, node capacitance and source (`sim-schematic.test.ts`).
Where a diode's or an inductor's current ends, the boundary between two
modes, the simulator agrees with ngspice within 10 ns in every circuit of
the SPICE comparison below.

## How it computes

- **Within an interval** the state (inductor current, capacitor voltages)
  obeys a linear differential equation. The simulator advances it with the
  exact solution of that equation (a matrix exponential) over sub-steps of
  one two-thousandth of the switching period, or shorter, so that one
  period of the circuit's fastest ring spans at least twenty sub-steps, down
  to one twenty-thousandth of the period. The fastest ring comes from the
  eigenvalues of every interval's equations: the capacitance across the
  switch, a small output capacitor with the inductance, the input bus. A
  circuit that rings so fast that a ring would still span fewer than three
  sub-steps is refused: a diode could turn on and off again between two
  sub-steps unseen. A start-up drawn without a steady state follows the
  same rule. The exponential is computed by scaling and squaring with a
  Padé approximant <Cite key="higham2005" />, and is formed, and squared,
  less the identity. Before that, the states are rescaled by powers of two,
  which is exact, until each state's couplings to the others are of similar
  size in both directions (balancing <Cite key="parlett1969" />): a
  tiny bus capacitance behind a source resistance that is not small would
  otherwise make the matrix far larger than its fastest mode, and the linear
  solve inside the approximant would then pivot on those large couplings,
  which costs the slower states their digits. So a time constant far shorter
  than a sub-step does not cost them.
- **Events** such as the diode current reaching zero or a clamp starting to
  conduct are located inside a sub-step on the same exact solution, by a
  search that keeps the event bracketed like bisection (regula falsi in its
  Illinois variant, which needs far fewer steps). When one event ends a
  sub-step early, the other conditions are checked again at that instant.
  A condition that was met earlier in the sub-step, but no longer holds at
  its end, happened first: for example, a node capacitance's rise that
  passes the diode's turn-on voltage just before its current ends at the
  peak. The gate edges fall exactly at the start of the period and at the
  duty ratio.
- **Averages, powers and extremes.** Within an interval every quantity the
  page reports is a straight (affine) function of the states, and between
  two samples the states follow the interval's equations exactly. So the
  averages, the powers, and the mean squares behind the rms values and the
  conduction losses are the exact integrals of that solution over every
  sub-step, not straight lines through the samples. They are taken from the
  exponential of a larger matrix built from the interval's equations
  <Cite key="vanloan1978" />, rescaled and formed less the identity like the
  steps, in each state's deviation from the first state the period visits in
  that interval, with the rate of change there summed in twice the
  precision. A quantity whose constant part dwarfs what varies, such as a
  battery's current behind a small resistance, then keeps the digits its
  samples give it: a unit in the last place of the battery's voltage over
  its resistance, a resolution that the current of the capacitor beside it
  shares. A transient faster than a sub-step, such as a time constant far
  shorter than one, is integrated as exactly as a slow one. Through the same
  samples, the results agree with a 60-digit integration to a few parts in a
  trillion of each quantity's scale in the tested circuits, including ones
  whose time constant is several hundred billion times shorter than a
  sub-step, and between two grids of sub-steps to about one part in ten
  billion. Over hundreds of random circuits, two grids from the same start
  agree as closely against the circuit's largest current or voltage, down to
  the battery current's resolution, wherever they see the same events; where
  the currents are themselves only rounding, as in a capacitor alone that has
  settled, they agree to that rounding. Where
  one grid sees a diode conduct briefly inside a sub-step and the other does
  not, they differ by what that conduction carries (see Outside the model
  below). Each quantity's largest and smallest value also include a peak
  between two samples: where its slope changes sign within a sub-step, and
  where the slope dips through zero and back within one (a ring riding on a
  steeper ramp). The slope is also checked close to the sub-step's start,
  where a fast transient decays. A turn within the rounding of the slope
  itself is not searched; a quantity that barely moves, at the rounding of
  its own value, is still searched where its slope turns, which makes a few
  circuits several times slower. Each slope is the rate of change at the
  sample, summed in twice the precision and carried along the sub-step like
  the states, so an input bus behind a tiny source resistance and
  capacitance, whose slope is what is left of terms many orders of magnitude
  larger, still has its peak placed to rounding. The plots draw the samples,
  so a peak narrower than a sub-step can be in the numbers but not in the
  plot.
- **Rounding.** Within a period the simulator follows each state's
  deviation from its value at the start of the period. Its rounding errors
  then scale with how far the state moves, not with its size, so the change
  over a period is exact to rounding even for a very large capacitor.
- **The periodic steady state** is found by Newton iteration on the map from
  one period's initial state to the next, with the map's exact derivative:
  the product of the sub-steps' matrices, corrected at every event whose time
  depends on the state. The simulator accumulates the derivative minus the
  identity, so a very slow state, whose sub-step matrix rounds to one, keeps
  its decay. It is accepted when two tests pass for every state, within at
  most two thousand periods:
  - the change over one period is below one part in a million of how far
    that state moves within the period. A change measured against the
    state's peak would not do: a current that grows by the same amount every
    period would pass once it had grown large enough;
  - the remaining Newton step, the estimated distance to the steady state,
    is below one part in a million of the state's size. The change alone
    would not do: a very large capacitor changes little per period even
    while it is still far from its steady state.

  Where the steady state is not a single point, the derivative shows it: a
  fixed output at exactly its balancing duty ratio, or a capacitor alone
  that no more charge reaches, has a state that neither moves the others
  nor is moved by them, and returns to whatever value it starts from. The
  Newton step leaves that state where it is and solves for the others; the
  first test alone says whether it still changes.

  A state resting at zero picks up the rounding of the voltages around it
  (a current of $10^{-20}$ A where the circuit's voltages are tens of volts),
  and that residue is then its whole movement within the period. So each
  state has a natural size in the circuit: the largest voltage the circuit
  is given (the input or the source's open-circuit voltage, a battery's, a
  fixed output's, the start voltage) for a voltage, and the current that
  voltage builds in one period in the state's inductance for a current. A
  change or a Newton step within rounding of that size counts as none, and
  the second test measures each state's step against the largest of its
  value, its movement within the period and that size.
- **The mode** is DCM when the inductor current stays at zero for part of
  the period, BCM when it only touches zero, and CCM otherwise, also when
  it reverses and flows on through the switch's body diode.

## Validation

The test suite (`packages/pe-core/test/sim.test.ts`) checks the simulator
against the equations:

- **Validation grid.** For the buck, boost, buck-boost and flyback, over a
  grid of duty ratios and values of $K$ on both sides of $K_\mathrm{crit}$:
  - the simulated conversion ratio and peak-to-peak inductor ripple are
    within 2 % of the equations;
  - the detected mode matches $K$ against $K_\mathrm{crit}$.
- **Energy balance.** With losses, the input energy per period equals the
  output energy plus the computed losses, also with a capacitance across
  the switch and tens of volts of ripple.
- **Newton's derivative.** The exact derivative of the period map, minus the
  identity, matches central differences of the change over a period, also
  for very slow states.
- **Very large capacitors.** A bus capacitor whose time constant spans
  billions of periods still settles at the loss-free-resistor divider, and
  a huge output capacitor gives the same output voltage as a much smaller
  one.
- **Ringing.** The DCM ringing frequency matches the L-C equation.
- **Source-driven flyback.** It pins its input at the critical voltage when
  the source can supply more than the DCM power there, and it settles below
  that voltage in DCM when the source cannot.
- **No steady state.** A buck fed into a fixed output above its balancing
  duty ratio reports the current's change per cycle, the average inductor
  voltage and the balancing duty ratio exactly; below it, and at it, the
  buck and the boost settle; a billionth above it the buck counts as
  balanced, a hundred-thousandth above it runs away; the buck-boost's
  balancing duty ratio comes from its inverted ratio; a forward converter
  above its reset limit reports its magnetizing current walking up, also
  into a fixed output or a capacitor alone.
- **A capacitor alone.** It charges without bound behind a boost. Behind a
  buck it settles at the input from below and from above, also with a
  capacitance across the switch, losses and a source. Behind a forward
  converter it keeps a start voltage above $n V_g$, the peak of an L-C
  charge that overshoots, and, above the reset limit with the magnetizing
  current held by the switch's resistance, the voltage its start-up stops
  at. With a capacitance across the switch, a boost or buck-boost stops
  where its start-up stops: also while the current left at turn-on still
  alternates from cycle to cycle, after a start-up that passes a lower
  voltage at which the periodic ringing would stop too, and while the input
  bus is still recovering. A search cut short reports that the capacitor
  has not settled.
- **The body diode at zero current.** When the diode's current ends with the
  output above the input (an L-C overshoot, a battery behind a resistor),
  the buck's current turns negative at once through the switch's body
  diode, and the switch voltage never falls below zero.
- **Outside the model.** The models hold each diode off outside the
  intervals in which it conducts, and take the switch's body diode as
  ideal. Two things are flagged, in the waveforms the page draws (the
  steady period with its extremes between samples, or the whole start-up):
  - a diode held off but forward-biased beyond its drop: the two-switch
    converters' diode D, or the forward converter's rectifier $D_1$,
    freewheeling diode $D_2$ or reset diode $D_3$. Examples: a buck, a
    buck-boost or a flyback with a heavy load on a source too weak for it;
    a forward converter beyond its reset limit on one; a forward converter
    whose switch drop, $R_\mathrm{on}$ times its current, exceeds a sagging
    bus while the switch is on, which forward-biases $D_2$ (and $D_3$ once
    the drop exceeds $(1 + 1/n_r)$ times the bus); a boost whose output
    falls below its input (held there by a fixed output or a battery, or
    pulled there by a load too heavy for it), whose current through
    $R_\mathrm{on}$ lifts the switch node above the output while the switch
    is on;
  - the switch voltage falling below zero, where the ideal body diode would
    conduct. Without a capacitance across the switch, the switch voltage
    follows the input bus in the idle interval of DCM, so a bus below zero
    there is flagged this way. With one, the model's clamp interval conducts
    through the body diode instead.

  A diode with the model's drop would conduct there, which the model leaves
  out, so the page says that the results may not hold. How much they change
  grows with the excess and with the current that diode would carry.
  Elsewhere a bus below zero turns on no diode: two buck-boosts and a boost
  whose buses sag below zero, with every diode still off, are not flagged,
  and ngspice with every diode in place agrees with them within the SPICE
  comparison's tolerance. In the tests' sweep of ordinary circuits, over
  every converter, load, source and node capacitance, nothing is flagged.
  Among random circuits, most flags are the cases above: a weak source
  whose bus falls below zero, or a boost whose output falls below its input.
  The rest come from a capacitance across the switch whose ring
  forward-biases a diode, or takes the switch below zero, within a sub-step
  and turns back before its end: a turn-on that the event search, which
  checks the conditions at each sub-step's end, does not see. A diode's
  excess over its drop counts from a ten-thousandth of the circuit's
  largest voltage, and so does the switch voltage's dip below zero: what a
  diode forward-biased by less would take changes the results by about as
  little, below the four digits the page shows.

Two further suites check the simulator by other means:

- **Against SPICE** (`packages/pe-core/test/sim-spice.test.ts`). Thirty-four
  circuits are written again as netlists from their schematics and run from
  rest in ngspice <Cite key="ngspice" />, an independent solver with its own
  integrator (`scripts/spice_crosscheck.py`). They cover every topology in
  CCM and DCM; every interval of the forward converter, with reset windings
  of the primary's turns and of half of them, and a battery that holds its
  rectifier off; the node capacitance's ringing, also with a battery; a
  Thevenin source; batteries with and without a resistor, and one above the
  input, whose current flows back through the switch's body diode; an
  output that an L-C overshoot lifts above the input, where the current
  turns back through the body diode as the diode's current ends; a fixed
  output; a runaway start-up; capacitors charging from empty, also behind a
  boost with a node capacitance; and capacitors alone that stop charging
  behind a boost and a buck-boost with a node capacitance, one of them
  after a start-up that passes a lower voltage where the periodic ringing
  would also stop. In the last period, each quantity's average, largest,
  smallest and end value, and its value at 40 instants across the period,
  agree within 0.5 % of the quantity's largest magnitude in these cases,
  plus what the SPICE parts add to a quantity that the ideal parts hold at
  zero all period: 5 mV for a voltage, 1 µA for a current <Cite key="ngspice" />.
  Four cases need that allowance: the switch voltage across a conducting
  body diode, 3.9 mV, and the leakage of a diode held off, 0.5 nA at most <Cite key="ngspice" />. The SPICE
  parts are near-ideal: switches with the case's on-resistance (a milliohm
  when it gives none) that take a nanosecond to switch, and diodes that
  drop a few millivolts more than the case's $V_F$; a battery's current, set
  by a small $R_b$, is the most sensitive to those millivolts. The forward
  converter's netlist also has a small RC snubber across its freewheeling
  diode, which the simulator does not, to keep ngspice's integrator stable
  when that diode turns off. Two things are left out of the comparison:
  the values within 20 ns of the simulator's events (a real edge and an
  ideal one differ by nanoseconds), and, with a node capacitance, the
  diode's largest current and, for the buck and the flyback, the input
  current's extremes, where ngspice's steep diode overshoots for a few
  picoseconds as it takes over the current. Where a current ends within
  the period (a diode's or an inductor's in DCM, the forward converter's
  magnetizing current at the end of its reset), the instant it falls
  through 5 % of its average agrees within 10 ns (3.4 ns at worst). CI
  reruns ngspice on every change and compares with the stored results.
- **Operating modes** (`sim-schematic.test.ts`, `sim-sequence.test.ts`,
  `src/lib/seqlayout.test.ts`). Kirchhoff's current law at every node of the
  drawn circuit at every sample, for every topology with each load, with and
  without a node capacitance and a source; the modes cover the period's
  intervals, and the states match hand-checked cases (a buck in CCM and DCM,
  a boost in DCM, a flyback whose ringing leaves a negative current for its
  body diode at turn-on, a forward converter's reset). Every mode, element
  and state has an English and a Korean text, and the drawing's labels,
  symbols, wires and arrows do not overlap for any circuit in either
  language.
- **Invariants** (`packages/pe-core/test/sim-invariants.test.ts`). For every
  converter with a resistor, a battery, both, a capacitor alone and a fixed
  output, ideal and with losses: in the steady state the inductor's
  volt-seconds and the output capacitor's charge balance, the capacitor's
  current is $C$ times the slope of the simulated output voltage, and the
  source's power equals what the resistor, the battery's open-circuit
  voltage and its internal resistance, or the fixed output take, plus the
  losses. Over a start-up from rest the source's energy equals the energy
  stored in the inductors and capacitors plus what the load took and the
  losses. The first two hold by construction in a periodic steady state, so
  they check the bookkeeping; the energy balances check the model.

## Limitations

- **No transition shapes.** There is no leakage inductance, reverse
  recovery or switching transition, so the simulator shows no turn-off
  spikes and no overlap loss.
- **Fixed duty ratio.** There is no control loop and no core loss.
- **No steady state, no tables.** When the circuit has no periodic steady
  state ([above](#when-there-is-no-steady-state)), the simulator shows the
  start-up and its explanation only. With a resistance in the path of a
  growing current such circuits do settle, at a current that only that
  resistance limits and that can be very large. For
  the forward converter's magnetizing current that is only the
  on-resistance: the winding resistance belongs to the output inductor.
- **A battery at one state of charge.** The simulator holds the battery's
  open-circuit voltage and resistance constant, and the Rint model has no
  polarization: the internal resistance is the battery's only loss.
- **A slow start-up.** A capacitor alone behind a forward converter, or
  behind a converter with a capacitance across its switch, is followed for
  at most 4000 periods and two million sub-steps before the search (100 to
  1000 periods with a node capacitance). A start-up slower than that, such
  as a slow L-C charge, is searched from where it got to. It is reported
  below the voltage at which it would stop, by up to about 0.07 % in random
  trials. A steady state found from above can instead lie a few millionths
  above that voltage. With a node capacitance that rings fast, such a
  circuit can take tens of seconds to solve. It can take about a minute
  when the search then does not settle.
- **Diodes only where they are expected.** Each diode, the switch's body
  diode among them, is part of the model only in the intervals in which it
  conducts. A weak source can let the bus sag, or a large current through
  $R_\mathrm{on}$ can lift a switch node or exceed the bus, until a diode
  the model holds off is forward-biased. The simulator then says that the
  results may not hold.
